## 1. Specify the average before choosing an approximation

Keep the book's constant value-plane regulator \(\epsilon>0\):

\[
H(t,z)=\int_0^\infty e^{tu^2}\Phi(u)\cos(zu)\,du,\qquad
\Phi(u)=\sum_{n\ge1}
(2\pi^2n^4e^{9u}-3\pi n^2e^{5u})e^{-\pi n^2e^{4u}},
\]
\[
D=|H|^2+\epsilon^2,\quad
A=\frac{\epsilon H_z}{\sqrt\pi D},\quad
\rho_\epsilon=|A|^2,\quad
W=2\partial_z^2\log D.
\]

The unknown of interest is \(\rho_\epsilon\), with its absolute magnitude.
The amplitude \(A\) and lift \(H\) are useful ways to model its transport.
For theta, \(\int_{\mathbb C}\rho_\epsilon=\infty\) at each real time;
the book proves this for this particular lift. Local averages are still
well defined.

Let \(K\) be a nonnegative, centered, smooth compactly supported spatial
kernel of integral one, and let \(k\) be a nonnegative, centered, smooth
compactly supported time kernel of integral one. Define

\[
K_\ell(w)=\ell^{-2}K(w/\ell),\quad
k_\tau(s)=\tau^{-1}k(s/\tau),\qquad
\bar\rho_{\epsilon,\ell,\tau}(t,z)
=\int_{\mathbb R}\int_{\mathbb C}
k_\tau(s)K_\ell(w)\rho_\epsilon(t-s,z-w)\,dA_w\,ds.
\tag{1}
\]

These unit-weight kernels average the density; they do not divide it by
its total mass. Either kernel can be replaced by a point mass to study
time averaging alone or spatial averaging alone. Different scales along
\(x\) and \(y\) are also natural: average along the real-height direction
while retaining the transverse shape. Compact support avoids importing
an unproved whole-plane Gaussian-integrability assumption.

Compare a small family of explicit averaging scales. The useful
question is whether a stable envelope emerges over a range of scales,
with its magnitude, transverse spread and time trend recorded. If the
shape changes substantially as the averaging window changes, report that
dependence rather than declaring a unique average.

## 2. Averaging preserves a usable exact evolution

The book gives

\[
j=\frac{\epsilon^2H_{zz}\overline{H_z}}{\pi D^2},\qquad
\partial_t\rho_\epsilon+2\Re(\partial_zj)=0.
\]

For fixed kernels and fixed \(\epsilon\), let \(\bar j\) be the same
space-time average of \(j\). Differentiation commutes with the compact
averages, giving exactly

\[
\partial_t\bar\rho+2\Re(\partial_z\bar j)=0.
\tag{2}
\]

Thus an average-shape model should predict both \(\bar\rho\) and its
current. Time averaging alone gives a legitimate instance of (2).
For a fixed spatial region \(\Omega\), retain the unnormalized balance
\[
\frac{d}{dt}\int_\Omega\bar\rho\,dA
=-\int_{\partial\Omega}\bar{\boldsymbol j}\cdot n\,ds.
\]
The required spatial and temporal buffer equals the averaging kernel's
support; do not silently truncate or renormalize it at the computational
boundary.

There is a closure problem: averaging the nonlinear amplitude equation
does not replace its products by products of averaged quantities.
If \(\mathcal A\) denotes the averaging operation in (1), then
\(\mathcal A(|A|^2)\ne|\mathcal A(A)|^2\) in general, and
\(\mathcal A((\overline W-W)A)\) contains correlations. Complex
conjugation and averaging must remain distinct operations.

The first route should average the density and current generated by an
approximate \(H\). A reduced equation directly for the envelope is a
second route, with its missing current/correlation terms measured.
If averaging widths depend on time or position, their kernel-derivative
terms must be added to (2).

## 3. The negative-time anchor: a Gaussian field and a simple mean shape

Put \(t=-T\), \(T\to+\infty\), and \(\phi_0=\Phi(0)>0\).
The canonical source is even and smooth at zero. On fixed compact
complex \(z\)-sets, its endpoint expansion gives

\[
H(-T,z)=\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2\sqrt T}
\left[\phi_0+
\frac{\Phi''(0)-\phi_0z^2}{4T}+O(T^{-2})\right].
\tag{3}
\]

One way to obtain (3) is to multiply the Taylor expansions of
\(\Phi(u)\) and \(\cos(zu)\), and integrate the powers against
\(e^{-Tu^2}\). Splitting the integral at fixed small \(u\) controls the
tail; the same argument applies to the needed spatial derivatives.
[Watson's lemma][watson] is the standard endpoint-expansion framework.
An explicit numerical remainder constant is a future deliverable.

A useful unexpanded leading model is

\[
H_G(-T,z)=C_Te^{-z^2/(4T)},\qquad
C_T=\frac{\phi_0\sqrt\pi}{2\sqrt T}.
\tag{4}
\]

This model satisfies \(H_t=-H_{zz}\) exactly on \(t<0\).
Its lift therefore satisfies the exact nonlinear field equations there.
Its source and its behavior through \(t=0\) differ from theta's; exact
evolution alone does not establish an approximation.

The corresponding density is

\[
\rho_G(-T,z)=
\frac{\epsilon^2 C_T^2|z|^2
e^{-\Re(z^2)/(2T)}}
{4\pi T^2
\left(C_T^2e^{-\Re(z^2)/(2T)}+\epsilon^2\right)^2}.
\tag{5}
\]

For fixed \(\epsilon\) and fixed compact \(z\)-sets,
\[
\rho_\epsilon(-T,z)=
\frac{\phi_0^2}{16\epsilon^2T^3}|z|^2+O(T^{-4}).
\tag{6}
\]
In particular the fixed-region density decreases like \(T^{-3}\).
This local limit is compatible with infinite global mass: it is not a
statement that the whole-plane mass tends to zero.

For the centered spatial kernel above, set
\(m_2=\int|w|^2K(w)\,dA_w\). With fixed compact averaging windows,
(6) gives

\[
\bar\rho_{\epsilon,\ell,\tau}(-T,z)=
\frac{\phi_0^2}{16\epsilon^2T^3}
\left(|z|^2+\ell^2m_2\right)+O(T^{-4}).
\tag{7}
\]

Time averaging over a fixed window leaves this leading law unchanged.
For windows comparable to \(T\), retain the integral of
\((T+s)^{-3}\) in (1) instead of substituting \(T^{-3}\), and keep the
whole averaging interval in the negative-time regime.

There is also a larger spatial regime \(z=\sqrt T\,\zeta\). The leading
Gaussian calculation predicts, on fixed compact \(\zeta\)-sets,
\[
T^2\rho_\epsilon(-T,\sqrt T\,\zeta)
\longrightarrow
\frac{\phi_0^2}{16\epsilon^2}|\zeta|^2
e^{-\Re(\zeta^2)/2}.
\tag{8}
\]
This follows by substituting \(u=v/\sqrt T\) in the source integral;
bounded \(\zeta\) permits Gaussian domination. It is an asymptotic
rescaling for comparison, not a normalization of the physical density.
Larger transverse windows require new remainder control; do not extend
the apparent transverse growth in (8) to the whole plane.

Higher negative-time approximants can be built from the even spatial
derivatives of the Gaussian transform, with coefficients
\((-1)^k\Phi^{(2k)}(0)/(2k)!\). Each term still solves the heat equation.
This gives a systematic way to improve the average envelope while
retaining the evolution exactly.

## 4. The positive-time anchor: average the oscillatory model analytically

The repository already has a [complete-source saddle analysis][saddle].
Write
\[
q_t(u)=tu^2+9u-\pi e^{4u},\quad
4\pi e^{4L}=2tL+9,\quad
\kappa=8tL-2t+36,\quad L'=\frac{2L}{\kappa}.
\tag{9}
\]
At large positive time, \(L\sim(\log t)/4\), and the source concentrates
near \(u=L\). A candidate field envelope is
\[
H_+(t,z)=M(t)e^{-z^2/(2\kappa(t))}\cos(L(t)z),
\qquad M(t)\approx H(t,0),
\tag{10}
\]
with leading saddle estimate
\[
M(t)\approx 2\pi^2e^{q_t(L)}\sqrt{2\pi/\kappa}.
\]
The source prefactor, skewness and higher moments provide corrections.
Use the complete-source estimates, rather than retaining the \(n=1\)
term without a tail budget. The existing theorem has fixed finite
real-root-label scope; complex-domain estimates and growing-height or
growing-population averages require additional work.

For the simplest slice model \(H=M\cos(Lz)\), its average along a
complete real spatial period is explicit. Put
\[
\eta=\epsilon/M,\quad Y=Ly,\quad a=\sinh^2Y+\eta^2.
\]
Then
\[
\begin{split}
\langle\rho\rangle_x(y)
&=\frac{L}{\pi}\int_{x_0}^{x_0+\pi/L}\rho(x+iy)\,dx\\
&=\frac{L^2\eta^2}{2\pi}
\frac{2a(a+1)+1-\eta^2(2a+1)}
{\{a(a+1)\}^{3/2}}.
\end{split}
\tag{11}
\]
The result is independent of \(x_0\), retains the absolute scale, and
describes the transverse average shape directly. It follows from
\[
\left\langle(a+\cos^2X)^{-1}\right\rangle
=[a(a+1)]^{-1/2}
\]
and differentiation with respect to \(a\). The
[small numerical check][check] compares (11) with direct period
quadrature; it checks this model identity, not theta approximation.

The exact cosine model has
\[
\int_{\mathbb R}\langle\rho\rangle_x(y)\,dy=L/\pi.
\tag{12}
\]
For example, average \(\log(|H|^2+\epsilon^2)\) first and use
\(\rho=(4\pi)^{-1}\Delta\log D\): its averaged \(y\)-derivative tends to
\(\pm2L\) at the two transverse ends, proving (12).
For a transverse averaging scale that does not resolve the regulator
layer, the small-\(\eta\) shape is the smoothed line measure
\[
\nu_{\mathrm{mean}}(t)=\frac{L(t)}{\pi}\,dx\,\delta_0(dy).
\tag{13}
\]
Thus a leading prediction to test is a mean line density growing like
\((\log t)/(4\pi)\), with the displayed transverse shape determined
by the regulator and the chosen averaging kernel. Equation (13) is a
cosine-model averaging limit. Transferring it to theta over an increasing
number of spatial periods is an open uniformity task, not a consequence
of the fixed-label theorem alone.

Time averaging can be applied directly to (11), retaining \(L(t-s)\)
and \(\eta(t-s)\) inside the integral. Averaging the parameters first
and inserting them into (11) generally gives a different answer.
For time averaging to sample many oscillations at a fixed real height,
the approximate phase change is \(|xL'(t)|\tau\). This should be large
compared with one cycle while the mean envelope varies slowly.
Near \(x=0\), such a temporal averaging mechanism need not operate.
There is no assumed equivalence between time and space averages and
no ergodicity claim.

## 5. Boundary information must retain the theta source

The two limits are useful anchors, but their leading behavior does not
uniquely specify the intermediate evolution. To see this, perturb the
source by a smooth even bump supported in \(a\le |u|\le b\), \(a>0\).
The perturbation changes \(H\) while still satisfying the same heat
equation. Its contribution at \(t=-T\) is \(O(e^{-a^2T})\) on fixed
complex sets, so it leaves every algebraic endpoint term unchanged.
At positive time it grows at most like \(e^{b^2t}\) on those sets,
below the dominant theta saddle scale. Small nonnegative bumps even
preserve source positivity. They fail the unchanged canonical-source
condition.

Consequently, use both asymptotic anchors together with the actual source
integral or a verified finite-time complex-field anchor. Do not fit an
arbitrary solution solely to the two limiting shapes.

The spatial conditions are equally concrete:

- Preserve evenness and conjugation of \(H\), hence reflection symmetry
  of \(\rho\) in both real coordinate axes.
- Supply finite-domain boundary data from the same complete source
  approximation, with sufficient buffers for the averages.
- Select \(W=2\partial_z^2\log(|H|^2+\epsilon^2)\) from that lift.
  A Poisson equation by itself leaves a harmonic ambiguity.
- Keep \(\epsilon\) fixed in the governing equations. Factoring out a
  large positive scale \(H=M F\) is useful numerically, but then the
  algebraic regulator is \(\eta=\epsilon/M\), with its time dependence
  retained. There is no change of target density.

The [earlier transport analysis][transport] also distinguishes stable
whole-real-line field smoothing toward smaller heat time from
ill-conditioned reconstruction of selected root coordinates. Our mean
density target should use the field/source information directly and
measure its own conditioning.

## 6. Three practical approximation routes

**A. Average source-based quadrature on bounded time slabs.**
Use a finite cosine representation
\[
\widetilde H(t,z)=\sum_j w_j e^{tu_j^2}\cos(zu_j)
\tag{14}
\]
with fixed nodes and weights on each slab, derived from the theta source.
It solves the heat equation exactly. Compute its density and current,
then average those quantities. Integrating the oscillatory or periodic
part analytically where possible may make mean-shape evaluation much
cheaper than a fine spatial mesh.

The source quadrature and omitted tail still need bounds, including the
whole complex and temporal averaging buffer. One fixed finite source
cannot reproduce both infinite-time asymptotics: its smallest and largest
frequencies eventually dominate. Use it on declared finite slabs, with
the endpoint asymptotic models providing the outer regimes.

**B. Match a small family of analytic envelope models.**
Use Gaussian derivatives at negative time and saddle/cumulant corrections
at positive time, with a source-anchored intermediate correction.
For a blending function \(\alpha(t)\), the residual of
\(\alpha H_-+(1-\alpha)H_+\) includes
\(\alpha'(H_--H_+)\). Retain this matching cost. Optimize errors in
the averaged density and current, instead of fitting individual zeros.

**C. Fit a reduced mean-current model.**
Once A or B supplies reliable averages, fit a low-dimensional
\((\bar\rho,\bar j)\) model satisfying (2), with unknown functions such
as longitudinal mean intensity, transverse width and slow time trend.
Compare several averaging widths and hold out time intervals.
This route might give the most useful simple shape, but its current
closure must be learned or derived from actual theta data.

For a holomorphic approximant \(G=\widetilde H\), let
\(R_H=G_t+G_{zz}\), \(D_G=|G|^2+\epsilon^2\), and construct
\(\widetilde A,\widetilde W\) by the same lift. Direct differentiation
gives the useful residual transfer
\[
r_A=\frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt\pi}
\left[\frac{(R_H)_z}{D_G}
-\frac{G_z(\overline G R_H+G\overline{R_H})}{D_G^2}\right],
\tag{15}
\]
where
\[
r_A=\widetilde A_t-\widetilde A_{\bar z\bar z}
+\widetilde A_{zz}
+(\widetilde W-\overline{\widetilde W})\widetilde A.
\]
The potential constraint is exact for this lift. The averaged continuity
defect is exactly the average of \(2\Re(\overline{\widetilde A}r_A)\).
This supplies an evolution-error budget without requiring peak fitting.
Zero residual must still be paired with a source/data comparison:
the Gaussian and every finite cosine sum already have zero heat residual
in their respective time domains.

## 7. What to measure and what to screen

The first benchmark should report errors for the same
\((\epsilon,\ell_x,\ell_y,\tau)\), domain and source budget:

1. Averaged density error in a local integral norm, supplemented by a
   maximum error for the smooth envelope.
2. Absolute mass in fixed observation regions and the boundary flux
   predicted by the averaged current.
3. Averaged evolution defect and source-approximation error separately.
4. Stability of the inferred shape under changed averaging widths.
5. Evaluation cost against direct averaging of the complete theta integral.

If an optional divisor or pair statistic is later introduced, retain its
own regulator and smoothing limit order from the book. It is not needed
for this opening average-shape investigation.

The following analytic premise screen uses the repository's
[control records][controls]; their numerical certificates were not rerun.

| Control | What survives | Premise unavailable for transfer to theta |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Polynomial double-event field \(H=z^2-2t\) | Lift, averaged continuity and smooth finite-regulator evolution through a collision | Canonical theta source and its two time asymptotics |
| Euler-factor controls, for arbitrary allowed factor parameters | Heat equation, positivity/evenness and generic averaging identities | Unchanged complete theta source; their source constants must be recomputed |
| Weighted-square and finite-prime controls | Local heat identities and the support/moment properties recorded for each | Complete canonical reflection/source identity; no all-real-time slice is assumed |
| Reflected super-Gaussian control | Reflection and generic heat/average identities | Theta square coefficients and canonical tail data |
| Smooth-shape and positive-primitive controls | The specific shape or primitive asymptotic data preserved by each construction | Unchanged arithmetic source; preserved shape alone does not exclude events |
| Fixed-event, finite-profile and RH-conditional close-source controls | Their recorded finite observations or closeness and generic evolution | The complete canonical source/profile or ordinary Dirichlet arithmetic, as specified in each source |
| Compact spectral bump above | Both leading time envelopes and exact heat evolution | The actual complete source or complete finite-time field anchor |

No control fails generic averaging or continuity. That is compatible
with the present objective: these identities organize an approximation,
not an argument for simplicity. Any later distinguishing inequality must
undergo a fresh quantitative control test.

## 8. Recommended first research round

1. **Fix the averaging experiment.** Specify a finite time/space domain,
   fixed \(\epsilon\), and spatial-only, temporal-only and combined
   averaging schedules. Keep the raw density scale.
2. **Make the negative-time estimates effective.** Bound the remainder
   in (3) and its differentiated forms, propagate it through the lift
   and averaging, and test (7) and the larger-window prediction (8).
3. **Test the positive-time mean model.** Compare (11), its transverse
   smoothing and its time average with complete-source calculations,
   within a declared saddle regime. Determine when the slow envelope
   correction in (10) matters.
4. **Construct the intermediate approximation.** Use source quadrature
   or a matched analytic representation and pay (15) and the
   source/data discrepancy. Endpoint shapes are constraints, not all
   the available data.
5. **Choose the simplest successful envelope.** Retain a model only
   with a measured or bounded averaged error over the declared windows,
   faithful current/mass balance, and a cost advantage or analytic gain.
   A failed candidate is a result about that candidate and regime.

The first useful output is a compact formula or small model for the
space-time averaged unnormalized density, with its domain, averaging
scales and error budget stated. A new manuscript or a global zero theorem
is not required to make this investigation useful.
