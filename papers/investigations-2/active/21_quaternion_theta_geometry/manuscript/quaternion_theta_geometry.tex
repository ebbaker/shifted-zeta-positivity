\begin{abstract}
We construct an entire left Fueter regular quaternion field from the first
spectral jet of the theta heat family, with Newman time retained as an
independent real parameter. Starting with the theta Mellin transform, we
derive the normalization relating this family to the Riemann xi function.
We state the classical global heat-flow inputs and prove the equivalence
between the Riemann hypothesis and nonvanishing of the quaternion field
at every real argument and positive time. A translation identity gives
an equivalent zero-free half-plane for one fixed quaternion field.
Two stationary Laguerre signs, a locally bounded loss formulation, and
a scalar vertical-convexity criterion are derived and translated with
explicit coordinate and multiplication conventions. Cauchy--Fueter
reproduction yields signed boundary correlations; harmonic currents and
norm identities retain their precise limitations. For the complete theta
source we prove an explicit norm gap on $0\le t\le1$, $|x|\le8$, and a
quantitative transfer from a full angular gluing defect to four physical
jets. Remote perturbation controls and endpoint cancellations identify
why absolute estimates and separate theta-index diagonals do not settle
the remaining all-height sign problem. No proof of the Riemann hypothesis
is claimed.
\end{abstract}

\section{Scope and conventions}\label{sec:scope}
The Riemann hypothesis concerns the zeros of a particular scalar entire
function. Quaternion regularity is a property of its constrained lift;
the arithmetic content remains the complete theta source. The central
equivalence proved here is
\[
 \RH\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 F(t,x)\ne0\quad\hbox{for all }t>0,\ x\in\R,
\]
where $F$ is the explicit field constructed below. This is pointwise
nonvanishing. It requires neither a positive lower bound uniform in all
heights nor a simplicity assertion at time zero.

Hamilton units satisfy
$\ii^2=\jj^2=\kk^2=\ii\jj\kk=-1$ and $\ii\jj=\kk$.
The complex field $\C_i=\R+\ii\R$ is identified with the usual complex
numbers. Quaternion components are written on the right of $\jj$:
$Q=A+\jj B$, $A,B\in\C_i$. In particular
$\jj A=\overline A\jj$. Quaternion conjugation is denoted by a bar;
when its argument is complex it is ordinary complex conjugation.
The real part of a quaternion is $\Rea Q$, its imaginary quaternion
part is $\Ima Q$, and $|Q|^2=\overline QQ$.

We keep the following roles distinct:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
Symbol & Role \\
\midrule
$t\in\R$ & independent Newman time \\
$z=x+iy\in\C_i$ & scalar spectral variable \\
$q=z_0+\jj z_1\in\HH$ & quaternion argument with independent complex coordinates \\
$\tau=t+\overline{z_1}$ & complex heat-time displacement inside the lift \\
$h_m=\partial_z^mH$ & scalar spectral derivative of order $m$ \\
$\Lag_\ell=h_{\ell+1}^2-h_\ell h_{\ell+2}$ & Laguerre form, $\ell=0,1$ \\
$n,m\ge1$ & theta-series indices when a double sum is displayed \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Derivative-order indices may be written $j$ or $\ell$; the quaternion
unit $\jj$ is always bold. All quaternion differential operators multiply
units on the left unless
a right action is stated. A prime on a scalar heat slice differentiates
its spectral argument, never its time parameter. An angular theta
argument used later will be called $y_{\rm ang}$ when a distinction is
needed. The known threshold and localization theorems are stated as
classical inputs; the deductions from them and every coordinate
translation used here are proved in the text. The theta estimates have
the explicitly bounded domains displayed in their statements.

\section{The scalar theta family and its normalization}
\label{sec:rh-foundations}

The quaternion construction begins with a fixed scalar family.  We derive
its normalization so that subsequent changes of coordinates cannot change
the meaning of the Riemann hypothesis.  Write
\[
 \vartheta(y)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}e^{-\pi n^2y},\qquad
 \vartheta_+(y)=\sum_{n\ge1}e^{-\pi n^2y},\qquad y>0.
\]
The Gaussian Fourier transform and Poisson summation give
\begin{equation}
 \vartheta(y)=y^{-1/2}\vartheta(1/y).
 \label{eq:rh-poisson}
\end{equation}
Consequently the balanced real-variable field
\[
 P(u)=e^u\vartheta(e^{4u}),\qquad
 A(u)=e^u\vartheta_+(e^{4u}),\qquad P(u)=e^u+2A(u)
\]
is even.  These series and their derivatives converge locally uniformly
on the real line, and in a complex neighborhood of every real point.
Differentiating the series gives
\begin{align}
 \Phi(u)&=\frac{P''(u)-P(u)}{16}
         =\frac{A''(u)-A(u)}8\nonumber\\
 &=\sum_{n\ge1}
 \left(2\pi^2n^4e^{9u}-3\pi n^2e^{5u}\right)
 e^{-\pi n^2e^{4u}}.
 \label{eq:rh-theta-source}
\end{align}
Thus the full real source is even and real analytic; in particular all
its odd derivatives at zero vanish.  For $u\ge0$, each summand is
positive, since its prefactor is
$\pi n^2e^{5u}(2\pi n^2e^{4u}-3)$.  Also
\begin{equation}
 0<\Phi(u)<4\pi^2e^{9u-\pi e^{4u}},\qquad u\ge0.
 \label{eq:rh-source-majorant}
\end{equation}
Indeed, after dropping the negative term, successive terms of
$n^4e^{-\pi n^2e^{4u}}$ have ratio at most $16e^{-3\pi}<1/2$.
The geometric sum proves \eqref{eq:rh-source-majorant}.

For $\operatorname{Re}s>1$, termwise integration gives the theta Mellin
identity
\[
 \pi^{-s/2}\Gamma(s/2)\zeta(s)
 =\int_0^\infty y^{s/2-1}\vartheta_+(y)\,dy.
\]
This is also the standard Mellin representation in \cite{dlmf}.
Splitting at one and using \eqref{eq:rh-poisson}, namely
$\vartheta_+(y)=(y^{-1/2}-1)/2+y^{-1/2}\vartheta_+(1/y)$,
gives
\begin{equation}
 \xi(s)=\frac12+\frac{s(s-1)}2
 \int_1^\infty
 \left(y^{s/2-1}+y^{(1-s)/2-1}\right)\vartheta_+(y)\,dy,
 \label{eq:rh-xi-mellin}
\end{equation}
where $\xi(s)=s(s-1)\pi^{-s/2}\Gamma(s/2)\zeta(s)/2$ in its
initial domain.  The integral on the right is entire in $s$, providing
analytic continuation and $\xi(s)=\xi(1-s)$.  The usual completion
cancels the trivial zeta zeros and its removable singularities; its
zeros are the nontrivial zeta zeros.  The Euler product in
$\operatorname{Re}s>1$ and the functional equation place these zeros
in $0\le\operatorname{Re}s\le1$.

Set $s=1/2+iz/2$ in \eqref{eq:rh-xi-mellin} and substitute
$y=e^{4u}$.  Since $s(s-1)=-(1+z^2)/4$, one obtains
\begin{equation}
 \xi(1/2+iz/2)=\frac12-(1+z^2)
 \int_0^\infty A(u)\cos(zu)\,du.
 \label{eq:rh-xi-cosine}
\end{equation}
Evenness of $P$ gives $A'(0)=-1/2$.  The field $A$ and its derivatives
decay double exponentially at positive infinity.  Two integrations by
parts therefore yield
\[
 \int_0^\infty A''(u)\cos(zu)\,du
 =-A'(0)-z^2\int_0^\infty A(u)\cos(zu)\,du.
\]
Using \eqref{eq:rh-theta-source} and \eqref{eq:rh-xi-cosine} proves
the precise normalization
\begin{equation}
 H(t,z):=\int_0^\infty e^{tu^2}\Phi(u)\cos(zu)\,du,
 \qquad
 H(0,z)=\frac18\xi\!\left(\frac12+\frac{iz}{2}\right).
 \label{eq:rh-H-normalization}
\end{equation}
It agrees with the heat-family convention in \cite{polymath}.
Under this affine change of variable, the critical line is precisely
real $z$, while the critical strip is contained in $|\operatorname{Im}z|\le1$.
Thus RH is equivalent to every zero of $H(0,\cdot)$ being real.

\subsection{Analytic properties and the physical heat sign}

On a compact subset of complex $(t,z)$, the integrands for every fixed
mixed derivative are bounded by a constant times
\[
 u^N\exp\{T u^2+(Z+9)u-\pi e^{4u}\},
\]
which is integrable.  Differentiation under the integral and local uniform
convergence prove joint entireness in $(t,z)$ and
\begin{equation}
 \partial_tH=-\partial_z^2H.
 \label{eq:rh-physical-heat}
\end{equation}
Increasing Newman time is therefore backward heat in the spectral
coordinate.  For real $t$, the function is real entire and even, with
Schwarz reflection $H(t,\bar z)=\overline{H(t,z)}$.  Positivity of
the source gives
\begin{equation}
 H(t,iy)=\int_0^\infty e^{tu^2}\Phi(u)\cosh(yu)\,du>0,
 \qquad t,y\in\mathbb R.
 \label{eq:rh-imaginary-nonzero}
\end{equation}
No real-time slice is identically zero, and every spatial zero has finite
multiplicity.

Each fixed-time slice has order at most one.  To see this directly, absorb
$|t|u^2+9u$ into $(\pi/2)e^{4u}+C_t$ in
\eqref{eq:rh-source-majorant}.  For $R\ge1$ the maximum on $|z|\le R$
is bounded by
\[
 C_t\int_0^\infty e^{Ru-(\pi/2)e^{4u}}\,du
 \le\frac{C_t}4(\pi/2)^{-R/4}\Gamma(R/4).
\]
Stirling's estimate consequently gives
$\log\max_{|z|\le R}|H(t,z)|\le C_t+C R\log(2+R)$.
This growth condition will be used in the real-rooted factorization;
it is not dispensable for the necessity of the whole-plane sign criteria.

\section{The imported global heat-flow inputs}
\label{sec:rh-global-inputs}

We state the classical results used below rather than attribute them to
quaternion regularity.  In the normalization
\eqref{eq:rh-H-normalization}, de Bruijn's strip contraction says that
zeros in $|\operatorname{Im}z|\le a$ at time $t_0$ lie in
$|\operatorname{Im}z|\le\sqrt{\max(a^2-2h,0)}$ at time $t_0+h$,
$h\ge0$.  Newman supplies a finite real threshold $\Lambda$ such that
$H(t,\cdot)$ has only real zeros exactly for $t\ge\Lambda$.
These statements are from \cite{debruijn,newman}; the conventions are
recorded in \cite{polymath}, Theorem~3.2 and its introduction.
The threshold endpoint is included: local uniform convergence and
Hurwitz's theorem preserve absence of zeros in every nonreal disk.
Consequently
\begin{equation}
 \mathrm{RH}\iff\Lambda\le0,\qquad \Lambda\le\frac12.
 \label{eq:rh-threshold-equivalence}
\end{equation}
Rodgers and Tao prove $\Lambda\ge0$ \cite{rodgerstao}, so RH is also
equivalent to $\Lambda=0$.  Their lower bound is not needed for the
collision argument below.

One further, genuinely uniform input is required: there is an absolute
$C>0$ such that for $0<t\le1/2$, a zero with
$|\operatorname{Re}z|\ge e^{C/t}$ is real.  This is
\cite{polymath}, Theorem~1.5(i), together with evenness for negative
real parts.  Separate, uncontrolled localization radii for individual
times would not suffice for the compactness argument.

\section{A finite collision criterion for RH}
\label{sec:rh-collision}

\begin{lemma}[Local Hermite splitting]\label{lem:rh-hermite}
Let $W_t=-W_{zz}$ be jointly holomorphic near $(T,a)$, real for real
$(t,z)$, and suppose $W(T,\cdot)$ has exact multiplicity $m\ge2$ at
real $a$.  If $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_m$ are the roots of the
probabilists' Hermite polynomial $\operatorname{He}_m$, its nearby zeros
satisfy
\begin{equation}
 a+\sqrt{2(t-T)}\,\lambda_k+O(|t-T|),\qquad 1\le k\le m.
 \label{eq:rh-hermite-roots}
\end{equation}
At least one is nonreal immediately before $T$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Put $W_\ell=\partial_z^\ell W(T,a)$.  The heat equation and convergent
Taylor expansion give
\[
 W(T+\delta,a+w)=\sum_{p,q\ge0}
 \frac{(-\delta)^p w^q}{p!q!}W_{2p+q}.
\]
Take $\delta=\eta^2$ and $w=\sqrt2\eta\lambda$.  Division by
$\eta^m$ extends holomorphically to $\eta=0$, with value
\[
 \frac{2^{m/2}W_m}{m!}\operatorname{He}_m(\lambda),\qquad
 \operatorname{He}_m(\lambda)=m!\sum_{p=0}^{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}
 \frac{(-1)^p\lambda^{m-2p}}{2^p p!(m-2p)!}.
\]
The identity
$\operatorname{He}_m(\lambda)=(-1)^m e^{\lambda^2/2}
(d/d\lambda)^m e^{-\lambda^2/2}$ and $m$ integrations by parts
give orthogonality to every lower-degree polynomial for the positive
Gaussian measure.  If there were fewer than $m$ real sign-changing
roots, multiplying their linear factors would give a lower-degree
polynomial with a nonzero, constant-sign Gaussian pairing with
$\operatorname{He}_m$, a contradiction.  Thus the Hermite roots are
distinct and real.
The holomorphic implicit-function theorem gives
$\lambda_k(\eta)=\lambda_k+O(\eta)$, proving
\eqref{eq:rh-hermite-roots}.  Rouch\'e's theorem on a fixed small disk
around $a$ shows these are precisely its $m$ nearby zeros.
For $t=T-h$ take $\eta=i\sqrt h$.  A nonzero Hermite root gives
imaginary displacement $\sqrt{2h}\lambda_k+O(h)$, which is nonzero
for small positive $h$.  At least one root is nonzero when $m\ge2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[A positive threshold has a finite collision]
\label{prop:rh-finite-collision}
If $\Lambda>0$, some real $a\ne0$ satisfies
$H(\Lambda,a)=H_x(\Lambda,a)=0$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Choose $\Lambda/2\le t_k<\Lambda$ tending to $\Lambda$, and a
nonreal zero $z_k$ at each $t_k$.  Strip contraction from time zero
gives $|\operatorname{Im}z_k|\le1$.  The uniform localization theorem
gives $|\operatorname{Re}z_k|<e^{2C/\Lambda}$.  A subsequence therefore
converges to a finite $z_*$.  Joint continuity gives
$H(\Lambda,z_*)=0$, and the threshold property makes $z_*=a$ real.
If this zero were simple, the holomorphic implicit-function theorem
would give a unique nearby zero for times near $\Lambda$.  Reflection
forces that zero to be real at real times, contradicting $z_k$.
Hence $H_x(\Lambda,a)=0$.  Equation
\eqref{eq:rh-imaginary-nonzero} excludes $a=0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Exact positive-time endpoint]\label{thm:rh-endpoint}
For the genuine theta family,
\begin{equation}
 \mathrm{RH}\iff
 \{(t,x)\in(0,\infty)\times\mathbb R:
 H(t,x)=H_x(t,x)=0\}=\varnothing.
 \label{eq:rh-no-joint-zero}
\end{equation}
It suffices to exclude these common zeros for $0<t\le1/2$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Under RH, the threshold theorem gives real-rootedness at every $t\ge0$.
A multiple real zero at $T>0$ would yield a nonreal zero at a slightly
earlier positive time by Lemma~\ref{lem:rh-hermite}, a contradiction.
Conversely failure of RH gives $0<\Lambda\le1/2$, and
Proposition~\ref{prop:rh-finite-collision} supplies the forbidden zero.
Zeros at times strictly beyond $1/2$ cannot be multiple by the same
splitting argument and de Bruijn's real-rootedness there.
\end{proof}
The criterion makes no simplicity assertion at time zero.  Its converse
uses uniform finite-height localization; no unproved assertion that
collisions cannot escape to infinity has been substituted for it.

\section{Stationary Laguerre observations and bounded loss}
\label{sec:rh-stationary}

For real $(t,x)$ write $h_j=\partial_x^jH$ and
\begin{equation}
 \mathscr L_j=h_{j+1}^2-h_jh_{j+2},\qquad
 \mathcal E=H^2+H_x^2.
 \label{eq:rh-laguerre-energy}
\end{equation}
Each $h_j$ obeys the same heat equation.  On a differentiable stationary
curve $h_{j+1}(t,\chi(t))=0$, the chain rule gives
\begin{equation}
 \frac12\frac d{dt}h_j(t,\chi(t))^2
 =-h_jh_{j+2}=\mathscr L_j.
 \label{eq:rh-stationary-evolution}
\end{equation}
We shall use the two levels
\begin{equation}
 H_x=0\Longrightarrow\mathscr L_0=-HH_{xx}\ge0,
 \qquad
 H_{xx}=0\Longrightarrow\mathscr L_1=-H_xH_{xxx}\ge0.
 \label{eq:rh-two-signs}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Necessity under real-rootedness}

If a real-time slice $f=H(t,\cdot)$ has only real zeros, evenness, the
order bound, and $f(0)>0$ give its paired Hadamard product
\begin{equation}
 f(z)=f(0)\prod_{\gamma>0}(1-z^2/\gamma^2),\qquad
 \sum_{\gamma>0}\gamma^{-2}<\infty,
 \label{eq:rh-paired-product}
\end{equation}
with multiplicities.  Pairing the genus-one factors cancels their linear
exponentials, and evenness removes the remaining exponential linear
factor.  Finite products $p_N$ are real-rooted and converge locally
uniformly, with every fixed derivative.  Rolle's theorem gives
real-rootedness of their derivatives.  For a real-rooted polynomial $p$,
away from its zeros,
\[
 \frac{(p')^2-pp''}{p^2}
 =\sum_\rho\frac{m_\rho}{(x-\rho)^2}\ge0.
\]
Continuity handles its zeros.  Applying this to $p_N^{(j)}$ and passing
to the limit proves $\mathscr L_j(t,x)\ge0$ for every real $x$ and
every fixed $j$.  Under RH this applies to all $t\ge0$, proving the
necessity of \eqref{eq:rh-two-signs} without assuming simplicity at time zero.

\subsection{The predecessor branch at every even multiplicity}

\begin{lemma}[Real stationary predecessor]\label{lem:rh-predecessor}
Let $W_t=-W_{xx}$ be real analytic near $(T,a)$ and have exact even
spatial multiplicity $m=2r$ there.  Put $W_k=\partial_x^kW(T,a)$.
For small $h=T-t>0$ there is a real analytic curve $\chi(h)$ with
\begin{align}
 \chi(h)&=a-\frac{W_{m+1}}{rW_m}h+O(h^2),
 &W_x(T-h,\chi(h))&=0,\label{eq:rh-predecessor-chart}\\
 W(T-h,\chi(h))&=\frac{W_m}{r!}h^r+O(h^{r+1}),
 &W_{xx}(T-h,\chi(h))&=\frac{W_m}{(r-1)!}h^{r-1}+O(h^r).
 \label{eq:rh-predecessor-jets}
\end{align}
In particular $WW_{xx}>0$ along the predecessor branch.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The heat Taylor expansion is
\[
 W(T-h,a+z)=\sum_{p,q\ge0}\frac{W_{2p+q}}{p!q!}h^pz^q.
\]
Set $z=hy$.  Because $W_k=0$ for $k<m$, the quotient
$h^{-r}W_x(T-h,a+hy)$ extends analytically to $h=0$; a nonzero
term requires $2p+q+1\ge2r$, hence $p+q\ge r$.  Its value at zero is
\[
 \frac{W_m}{(r-1)!}y+\frac{W_{m+1}}{r!}.
\]
Its derivative in $y$ is nonzero.  The real analytic implicit-function
theorem gives $y(h)$ and $\chi(h)=a+hy(h)$, proving
\eqref{eq:rh-predecessor-chart}.  Substituting in the convergent Taylor
series proves \eqref{eq:rh-predecessor-jets} and
\[
 WW_{xx}=\frac{W_m^2}{r!(r-1)!}h^{2r-1}+O(h^{2r})>0.
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Two signs exclude finite collisions]
\label{thm:rh-stationary-local}
Suppose \eqref{eq:rh-two-signs} holds throughout an open set
$\Omega\subset(0,\infty)\times\mathbb R$.  Then the genuine theta
family has no common zero of $H,H_x$ in $\Omega$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Any such zero has finite multiplicity $m\ge2$.  If $m$ is even,
Lemma~\ref{lem:rh-predecessor} applied to $W=H$ supplies a branch
with $H_x=0$ and $HH_{xx}>0$, contradicting the first sign.  If
$m=2r+1\ge3$, apply the lemma to $W=H_x$, of even multiplicity $2r$.
Its branch has $H_{xx}=0$ and $H_xH_{xxx}>0$, contradicting the
second sign.  Openness keeps these predecessor branches inside $\Omega$;
take $h<T$ so their times remain positive.
\end{proof}
This argument applies to any real analytic heat solution whose candidate
zeros have finite spatial multiplicity.  Both levels are needed:
$W(t,x)=x^3-6(t-T)x$ has $\mathscr L_0=3x^4+36(t-T)^2\ge0$, yet
a triple collision at $(T,0)$; its second stationary sign fails before
$T$.  At a common zero, the products $HH_{xx}$ and $H_xH_{xxx}$ vanish.
Checking only the collision point therefore loses the contradiction on
its predecessor branch.  At a double zero, $\mathscr L_1=H_{xx}^2>0$;
it is not a point of the second stationary set.

\begin{theorem}[Locally bounded stationary loss]
\label{thm:rh-bounded-loss}
On an open positive-time domain $\Omega$, suppose nonnegative functions
$K_0,K_1$ are locally bounded, including at every candidate common zero,
and satisfy
\begin{equation}
 H_x=0\Longrightarrow\mathscr L_0\ge-K_0\mathcal E,
 \qquad
 H_{xx}=0\Longrightarrow\mathscr L_1\ge-K_1\mathcal E.
 \label{eq:rh-bounded-loss}
\end{equation}
Then no common zero occurs in $\Omega$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
At an even collision $m=2r$, the predecessor branch has $H_x=0$,
$\mathcal E=H^2>0$, and
\[
 \frac{-\mathscr L_0}{\mathcal E}
 =\frac{H_{xx}}H=\frac r h+O(1).
\]
At an odd collision $m=2r+1$, its derivative predecessor branch has
$H_{xx}=0$.  Lemma~\ref{lem:rh-predecessor} gives
$H_x=c h^r+O(h^{r+1})$ and
$H_{xxx}=r c h^{r-1}+O(h^r)$, $c\ne0$.
The Taylor series for $H$ at $z=\chi(h)-a=O(h)$ has no term of
degree less than $r+1$ in $h$, because $2p+q\ge2r+1$ forces
$p+q\ge r+1$.  Hence $H=O(h^{r+1})$ and
$\mathcal E=H_x^2(1+O(h^2))>0$.  Therefore
\[
 \frac{-\mathscr L_1}{\mathcal E}
 =\frac{H_xH_{xxx}}{H^2+H_x^2}=\frac r h+O(1).
\]
Either divergence contradicts the assumed local boundedness of the
corresponding $K_j$.  All divisions are on nonzero predecessor branches,
not at the common zero itself.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Global stationary criteria]\label{cor:rh-global-signs}
For the genuine theta family, RH is equivalent to
\eqref{eq:rh-two-signs} for every $t>0,x\in\mathbb R$.  It is also
equivalent to existence of nonnegative locally bounded $K_0,K_1$ on
$(0,\infty)\times\mathbb R$ satisfying \eqref{eq:rh-bounded-loss}.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Necessity follows from the paired polynomial approximation, with
$K_0=K_1=0$.  Sufficiency combines either local exclusion theorem with
Theorem~\ref{thm:rh-endpoint}.
\end{proof}
Limited-domain estimates exclude only collisions in their covered
predecessor neighborhoods.  No estimate obtained by dividing by the
unproved physical norm establishes bounded loss at its possible zeros.

An equivalent single constrained observation uses the norm's time
derivative.  Directly from \eqref{eq:rh-physical-heat},
\[
 \partial_t\mathcal E=-2(HH_{xx}+H_xH_{xxx}).
\]
It equals $2\mathscr L_0$ on $H_x=0$ and $2\mathscr L_1$ on
$H_{xx}=0$.  Thus the two stationary signs are precisely
\begin{equation}
 \partial_t\mathcal E\ge0\quad\hbox{where }H_xH_{xx}=0.
 \label{eq:rh-unified-stationary}
\end{equation}
Likewise \eqref{eq:rh-bounded-loss} is equivalent to existence of one
nonnegative locally bounded $K$ with
$\partial_t\mathcal E\ge-2K\mathcal E$ on that union; take
$K=\max(K_0,K_1)$ in one direction and $K_0=K_1=K$ in the other.

\section{Whole-plane scalar curvature criteria}
\label{sec:rh-complex-curvature}

These are the classical complex Laguerre criteria
\cite[Theorems 2.3--2.4]{csordasescassut}; the following proof
uses the specific even, order-at-most-one theta class rather than any
unstated restriction on a general entire function.
Fix real time $t$ and set $f(z)=H(t,z)$,
$M(x,y)=|f(x+iy)|^2$.  Componentwise complex differentiation gives
\begin{equation}
 M_y=-2\operatorname{Im}(f'\bar f),\qquad
 \frac12M_{yy}=|f'|^2-\operatorname{Re}(f''\bar f)
 =:\mathcal C_0(t,x+iy).
 \label{eq:rh-Hermitian-curvature}
\end{equation}
On $y=0$ this equals $\mathscr L_0$, but away from that slice it
is a real Hermitian observation rather than the complex bilinear
$f'^2-ff''$.

\begin{theorem}[Vertical scalar criteria]\label{thm:rh-vertical}
For a fixed real theta time, the following conditions are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
 \item all zeros of $H(t,\cdot)$ are real;
 \item $M_y(x,y)\ge0$ for all real $x$ and $y>0$;
 \item $M_{yy}(x,y)\ge0$ for all real $x,y$;
 \item $-\operatorname{Im}(f'(z)/f(z))\ge0$ for every
 upper-half-plane point where the quotient is defined.
\end{enumerate}
In particular each condition at $t=0$ is equivalent to RH.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Under real-rootedness, \eqref{eq:rh-paired-product} supplies finite
real-rooted polynomials converging with their derivatives.  Their squared
moduli on a vertical line are positive constants times products of
$((x-\rho)^2+y^2)$.  Each is a polynomial in $y^2$ with nonnegative
coefficients, nondecreasing for $y>0$ and convex for all real $y$.
Local derivative convergence passes both inequalities to $M$.

Conversely suppose $f(a+ib)=0$, $b>0$.  Monotonicity and nonnegativity
force $M(a,y)=0$ for $0<y<b$, contradicting the identity theorem.
For the convexity condition, reflection also gives $M(a,-b)=0$;
convexity between the two zero endpoints, together with nonnegativity,
forces the same contradiction.  Reflection supplies an upper-half-plane
zero whenever any nonreal zero exists.

Away from zeros, $M_y/(2M)=-\operatorname{Im}(f'/f)$.
A zero $a+ib$ of multiplicity $m$ produces the pole
$m/(z-a-ib)$ plus a locally bounded holomorphic term.  At
$z=a+i(b+\varepsilon)$ its negative imaginary part is
$m/\varepsilon+O(1)$, which changes sign for small positive and
negative $\varepsilon$, both still inside the upper half-plane.
Thus the fourth condition also excludes all nonreal zeros and is
equivalent to the second.
\end{proof}
The growth condition was used only for real-rootedness to imply these
signs.  It matters: $e^{z^2}$ is real and zero-free but its vertical
modulus decreases.  Convexity or monotonicity excludes nonreal zeros
without a growth assumption.  For the theta family the exact endpoint is
\begin{equation}
 \mathrm{RH}\iff\mathcal C_0(0,z)\ge0
 \quad\hbox{for every }z\in\mathbb C.
 \label{eq:rh-whole-plane-criterion}
\end{equation}
Reality and source positivity make the pure imaginary axis positive
already; the equivalent condition requires every translated vertical
line.  Under real-rootedness the logarithmic derivative also satisfies
\[
 -\operatorname{Im}\frac{f'(x+iy)}{f(x+iy)}
 =\sum_{\rho\in\mathbb R}\frac{m_\rho y}{(x-\rho)^2+y^2},\qquad y>0.
\]
This partial-fraction representation is a consequence of the real-zero
assumption, not an unconditional arithmetic representation for proving it.

For the trace $f_{\mathrm{jet}}=H+\mathbf jH_z$, the scalar observation
in \eqref{eq:rh-whole-plane-criterion} cannot be replaced by its total
norm.  Indeed
$\tfrac12\partial_y^2|f_{\mathrm{jet}}|^2=\mathcal C_0+\mathcal C_1$,
where $\mathcal C_1=|H_{zz}|^2-\operatorname{Re}(H_{zzz}\overline{H_z})$.
The heat solution $H(t,z)=z^2-2t+1$ has nonreal time-zero roots,
but its total-norm curvature is $2x^2+6y^2+2>0$.  This distinguishes
the scalar curvature criterion from an unsigned jet-norm estimate.

\section{Hamilton conventions and a regular lift with independent time}
\label{sec:q21-conventions}

We use Hamilton units satisfying
\[
 \mathbf i^2=\mathbf j^2=\mathbf k^2=-1,
 \qquad \mathbf i\mathbf j=\mathbf k,
 \quad \mathbf j\mathbf k=\mathbf i,
 \quad \mathbf k\mathbf i=\mathbf j.
\]
The complex field is the subalgebra
\(\mathbb C_{\mathbf i}=\mathbb R+\mathbf i\mathbb R\).
Complex coefficients are placed on the right of \(\mathbf j\).
For complex numbers \(a,b,c,d\), direct Hamilton multiplication gives
\begin{equation}
 (a+\mathbf jb)(c+\mathbf jd)
 =ac-\overline b d+\mathbf j(bc+\overline a d),
 \qquad
 \overline{a+\mathbf jb}=\overline a-\mathbf jb.
 \label{eq:q21-product}
\end{equation}
Consequently \(|a+\mathbf jb|^2=|a|^2+|b|^2\).
Throughout this section an overline on a quaternion is quaternion
conjugation, and an overline on a complex coefficient is complex
conjugation. These operations agree on \(\mathbb C_{\mathbf i}\).

Write the independent quaternion coordinates as
\begin{equation}
 q=z_0+\mathbf jz_1,
 \qquad z_0=a+\mathbf ib,
 \quad z_1=c+\mathbf id.
 \label{eq:q21-coordinates}
\end{equation}
Since \(\mathbf j\mathbf i=-\mathbf k\), this is
\(q=a+\mathbf ib+\mathbf jc-\mathbf kd\).
Thus the oriented real Hamilton coordinates are
\((q_0,q_1,q_2,q_3)=(a,b,c,-d)\).
The normalized left Cauchy--Riemann--Fueter operator and its conjugate
are
\begin{align}
 \overline\partial_q
 &=\tfrac12(\partial_{q_0}+\mathbf i\partial_{q_1}
       +\mathbf j\partial_{q_2}+\mathbf k\partial_{q_3})
 =\tfrac12(\partial_a+\mathbf i\partial_b
       +\mathbf j\partial_c-\mathbf k\partial_d),
 \label{eq:q21-fueter-operator}\\
 \partial_q
 &=\tfrac12(\partial_{q_0}-\mathbf i\partial_{q_1}
       -\mathbf j\partial_{q_2}-\mathbf k\partial_{q_3}).
 \label{eq:q21-conjugate-operator}
\end{align}
All units in these expressions multiply on the left. A field is
left Fueter regular if \(\overline\partial_qF=0\).
This is the usual Hamilton orientation; the minus sign on
\(\partial_d\) comes solely from \(q_3=-d\).
The real definition and classical integral theory are reviewed in
\cite{sudbery,frenkellibine}.

For complex-valued differentiable functions \(U,V\), the coordinate
calculation is particularly useful. The \(a,b\) terms in
\(2\overline\partial_q(U+\mathbf jV)\) equal
\(2U_{\bar z_0}+2\mathbf jV_{z_0}\), and the \(c,d\) terms equal
\(-2V_{z_1}+2\mathbf jU_{\bar z_1}\). Hence
\begin{equation}
 \overline\partial_q(U+\mathbf jV)
 =U_{\bar z_0}-V_{z_1}
       +\mathbf j(V_{z_0}+U_{\bar z_1}).
 \label{eq:q21-component-fueter}
\end{equation}
Here subscripts denote ordinary Wirtinger derivatives of the complex
coefficients. When applied to a quaternion field, complex coordinate
derivatives always act componentwise:
\((U+\mathbf jV)_{z_0}=U_{z_0}+\mathbf jV_{z_0}\).
They are not defined by left multiplication of the whole field by
\(\mathbf i\) in a Wirtinger operator. This derivation fixes the
convention without importing complex coordinate formulas from a
different placement of coefficients.

Let \(H(\tau,z)\) be the jointly entire theta heat family defined
earlier, with
\(H_\tau=-H_{zz}\). The real Newman time \(t\) remains an independent
external parameter. Define
\begin{equation}
 \tau=t+\overline{z_1},\qquad
 F(t,q)=H(\tau,z_0)+\mathbf jH_z(\tau,z_0).
 \label{eq:q21-adopted-lift}
\end{equation}
Both quaternion coordinates are independent. The second coordinate
enters through a complex heat-time translation; it does not evaluate
the derivative at an independent spectral point.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:q21-regular-lift}
For every fixed real \(t\), the field \(F(t,\cdot)\) is globally
defined, real analytic, and left Fueter regular. It obeys
\[
 U_{z_0}=V,\qquad
 F_t=F_{\bar z_1}=-F_{z_0z_0},
 \qquad U=H(\tau,z_0),\ V=H_z(\tau,z_0).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Joint entireness of \(H\) makes the composition in
\eqref{eq:q21-adopted-lift} real analytic on all quaternion space.
Holomorphy gives \(U_{\bar z_0}=V_{z_1}=0\), while
\(V_{z_0}=H_{zz}\) and \(U_{\bar z_1}=H_\tau\).
Equation \eqref{eq:q21-component-fueter} therefore becomes
\(\overline\partial_qF=\mathbf j(H_{zz}+H_\tau)=0\).
The remaining identities follow by differentiating the declared lift.
\end{proof}

The extension is part of the construction, not a general uniqueness
claim for Fueter fields with a given trace. For instance, adding an
entire scalar \(g(z_1)\), with \(g(0)=0\), preserves regularity, the
physical trace, the jet relation, and the external heat equation.
It generally changes the declared antiholomorphic dependence on
\(z_1\), so later identities requiring that dependence apply to
\eqref{eq:q21-adopted-lift}, not to every such extension.
In contrast, the separated expression
\(H(t,z_0)+\mathbf jH_z(t,z_1)\) has regularity defect
\(-H_{zz}(t,z_1)\); at real \(q=x\), evenness gives
\(H_z(t,0)=0\), so that expression also loses the physical first jet.

The operators satisfy
\begin{equation}
 \partial_q+\overline\partial_q=\partial_{q_0},
 \qquad
 4\partial_q\overline\partial_q
 =4\overline\partial_q\partial_q=\Delta_4.
 \label{eq:q21-factorization}
\end{equation}
The anticommutation of distinct Hamilton units cancels mixed second
derivatives in the product, and each diagonal imaginary term contributes
\(+\partial_{q_\nu}^2\). On a regular field,
\(\partial_qF=\partial_{q_0}F\).
Every real coordinate derivative preserves regularity, so induction gives
\begin{equation}
 G_m:=\partial_q^mF=\partial_{q_0}^mF
 =h_m+\mathbf jh_{m+1},\qquad
 h_m=\partial_z^mH(\tau,z_0),\qquad
 \overline\partial_qG_m=0.
 \label{eq:q21-regular-jets}
\end{equation}
Holomorphy in \(z_0\) identifies the \(q_0\) derivative with the
componentwise spectral derivative. The external evolution is therefore
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\overline\partial_qF=0,\qquad F_t=-\partial_q^2F.}
 \label{eq:q21-quaternion-heat}
\end{equation}
These equations retain the declared theta trace and jet constraint;
regularity by itself does not impose either of them.

For comparison, on the physical complex pair \(u=H(t,z)\),
\(v=H_z(t,z)\), the jet and heat equations can also be packaged by
the matrix differential operator
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal D=
 \begin{pmatrix}\partial_z&-1\\-\partial_t&-\partial_z\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad
 \mathcal D\binom uv
 =\binom{u_z-v}{-u_t-v_z}=0,
 \qquad
 \mathcal D^2=(\partial_t+\partial_z^2)I.
 \label{eq:q21-complex-pair-square}
\end{equation}
The off-diagonal entries of the square vanish because constant
coefficient derivatives commute: the upper-right entry is
\(-\partial_z+\partial_z=0\), and the lower-left entry is
\(-\partial_t\partial_z+\partial_z\partial_t=0\).
Both diagonal entries are \(\partial_t+\partial_z^2\).
This is an operator on a two-dimensional complex vector, with matrix
endomorphisms rather than fixed Hamilton units. It is distinct from
the Euclidean Fueter factorization in \eqref{eq:q21-factorization};
neither factorization alone asserts a selfadjoint realization or a
positive physical observation.

\section{Physical trace, RH translation, and signed observations}
\label{sec:q21-physical-observations}

At a real quaternion argument \(q=x\), one has \(z_0=x,z_1=0\).
Since \(H(t,x)\) and its derivatives are real,
\begin{equation}
 F(t,x)=H(t,x)+\mathbf jH_x(t,x),\qquad
 \mathcal E(t,x):=|F(t,x)|^2=H(t,x)^2+H_x(t,x)^2.
 \label{eq:q21-physical-trace}
\end{equation}
Thus \(F(t,x)=0\) is exactly the simultaneous scalar condition
\(H(t,x)=H_x(t,x)=0\), namely a multiple real zero of the scalar
heat family. A simple zero of \(H\) is not a zero of \(F\).

The scalar positive-time collision theorem for the genuine theta family,
proved in the scalar part of this manuscript, consequently translates to
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\mathrm{RH}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 F(t,x)\ne0\quad\hbox{for all }t>0,\ x\in\mathbb R.}
 \label{eq:q21-rh-real-quaternion}
\end{equation}
No assertion of nonvanishing throughout quaternion space is made, and
the restriction \(t>0\) permits multiple scalar zeros at time zero.
The quaternion statement uses the scalar collision theorem rather than
deducing that theorem from regularity.

The external parameter has an exact translation covariance:
\begin{equation}
 F(t,q)=F(0,q+\mathbf jt).
 \label{eq:q21-time-translation}
\end{equation}
Indeed \(q+\mathbf jt=z_0+\mathbf j(z_1+t)\), and real \(t\)
commutes with conjugation, giving
\(\overline{z_1+t}=\bar z_1+t\).
For \(\mathcal F(q)=F(0,q)\),
\eqref{eq:q21-rh-real-quaternion} becomes
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\mathrm{RH}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \mathcal F(x+\mathbf js)\ne0
 \quad(x\in\mathbb R,\ s>0).}
 \label{eq:q21-rh-fixed-field}
\end{equation}
This is a statement on the positive \(\mathbf j\) half-plane of one
explicit entire Fueter field. In the original definition, \(t\) remains
an independent parameter; translation covariance merely permits the
equivalent fixed-field description. The real coordinate \(x\) of that
half-plane is spectral position, not Newman time.

For the complex observations define
\(\Lag_\ell=h_{\ell+1}^2-h_\ell h_{\ell+2}\).
Applying \eqref{eq:q21-product} to the regular jets gives
\begin{equation}
 \overline{G_\ell}G_{\ell+1}
 =\overline{h_\ell}h_{\ell+1}
   +\overline{h_{\ell+1}}h_{\ell+2}-\mathbf j\Lag_\ell.
 \label{eq:q21-laguerre-product}
\end{equation}
The scalar complex products are Hermitian, whereas the
\(\mathbf j\) coefficient is the un-conjugated complex Laguerre
polynomial. On the physical real slice all \(h_\ell\) are real and
\begin{equation}
 \Lag_\ell(t,x)
 =\operatorname{Re}\bigl(\mathbf j\overline{G_\ell(t,x)}G_{\ell+1}(t,x)\bigr)
 =-[\overline{G_\ell(t,x)}G_{\ell+1}(t,x)]_{\mathbf j}.
 \label{eq:q21-signed-projection}
\end{equation}
Here \([Q]_{\mathbf j}\) denotes the real coefficient of
\(\mathbf j\) in the Hamilton expansion of \(Q\).
Away from the real physical slice \(\Lag_\ell\) is generally
complex, so \(\operatorname{Re}(\mathbf j\overline{G_\ell}G_{\ell+1})\)
recovers only its real part.

The scalar projection of the field is also algebraic:
\begin{equation}
 P_0(A+\mathbf jB)=A,
 \qquad P_0Q=\tfrac12(Q-\mathbf iQ\mathbf i).
 \label{eq:q21-scalar-projection}
\end{equation}
It is a projection onto \(\mathbb C_{\mathbf i}\), not onto the real
part alone. On the real physical slice its value is real.

The heat equation gives the exact norm evolution
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal E_t=-2(h_0h_2+h_1h_3),\qquad
 (\partial_t+\partial_x^2)\mathcal E=2(h_1^2+h_2^2).
 \label{eq:q21-norm-time}
\end{equation}
The latter follows by differentiating \(h_0^2+h_1^2\) twice in
\(x\) and using \(h_{\ell,t}=-h_{\ell+2}\).
In particular,
\begin{equation}
 h_1=0\Longrightarrow\mathcal E_t=2\Lag_0,
 \qquad
 h_2=0\Longrightarrow\mathcal E_t=2\Lag_1.
 \label{eq:q21-stationary-norm}
\end{equation}
Thus the two stationary sign hypotheses are exactly equivalent to
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal E_t\ge0\quad\hbox{on}\quad
 \{(t,x):h_1(t,x)h_2(t,x)=0\}.
 \label{eq:q21-stationary-union}
\end{equation}
The same set is
\((P_0\partial_qF)(P_0\partial_q^2F)=0\) on the physical slice.
A single nonnegative locally bounded function \(K\) gives both
bounded-loss hypotheses if
\(\mathcal E_t\ge-2K\mathcal E\) on that union.
Conversely, from separate coefficients \(K_0,K_1\) one can take
\(K=\max(K_0,K_1)\), wherever both are locally bounded.
These reformulations retain the domain and predecessor coverage needed
by the collision-exclusion theorem. The positive source term in
\eqref{eq:q21-norm-time} is a backward-heat identity and supplies no
forward minimum principle.

The alternative whole-plane curvature criterion must retain the scalar
projection. On the complex slice \(z_1=0\), put \(z=x+\mathbf iy\).
Then \(P_0F(t,z)=H(t,z)\), whereas
\(|F(t,z)|^2=|H(t,z)|^2+|H_z(t,z)|^2\). Define
\[
 \mathcal C_\ell(t,z)
 =|h_{\ell+1}(t,z)|^2
   -\operatorname{Re}\bigl(h_{\ell+2}(t,z)\overline{h_\ell(t,z)}\bigr).
\]
Since \(\partial_yh_\ell=\mathbf i h_{\ell+1}\) and
\(\partial_y^2h_\ell=-h_{\ell+2}\), differentiating the scalar squared
modulus gives
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal C_0(t,z)=\tfrac12\partial_y^2|P_0F(t,z)|^2,
 \qquad
 \tfrac12\partial_y^2|F(t,z)|^2
       =\mathcal C_0(t,z)+\mathcal C_1(t,z).
 \label{eq:q21-projected-curvature}
\end{equation}
Consequently the scalar whole-plane criterion for this theta growth
class translates to
\begin{equation}
 \mathrm{RH}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \partial_y^2|P_0F(0,x+\mathbf iy)|^2\ge0
       \quad\hbox{for all }x,y\in\mathbb R.
 \label{eq:q21-rh-projected-curvature}
\end{equation}
The classical complex Laguerre criterion is stated in
\cite[Theorem~2.4]{csordasescassut}; the growth and scalar RH
identifications are supplied in the scalar part of this manuscript.
Replacing the projection by the total quaternion norm would change
the assertion. Indeed \(P(\tau,z)=z^2-2\tau+1\) has at \(t=0\)
\[
 \mathcal C_0=2x^2+6y^2-2,\qquad
 \mathcal C_1=4,\qquad
 \tfrac12\partial_y^2|F_P(0,z)|^2=2x^2+6y^2+2>0,
\]
although \(P(0,z)\) has the nonreal zeros \(\pm\mathbf i\).

\section{Cauchy--Fueter reconstruction, residuals, and boundary moments}
\label{sec:q21-boundary-formulas}

Fix real \(t\). Let \(\Omega\) be a bounded domain in
\(\mathbb H\), with piecewise \(C^1\) boundary, and let \(n(p)\)
be its outward unit normal expressed in the oriented Hamilton basis.
Set
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal K(r)=\frac{\bar r}{2\pi^2|r|^4}
 \qquad(r\ne0),\qquad D=2\overline\partial_q.
 \label{eq:q21-cauchy-kernel}
\end{equation}
For smooth quaternion fields \(A,B\), the real divergence theorem,
with the multiplication order retained, is
\begin{equation}
 \int_{\partial\Omega}B(p)n(p)A(p)\,dS_p
 =\int_\Omega\bigl((B D)A+B(D A)\bigr)\,dV_p,
 \label{eq:q21-bilinear-stokes}
\end{equation}
where \(B D=\sum_{\mu=0}^3(\partial_{q_\mu}B)e_\mu\)
is the right operator, with
\((e_0,e_1,e_2,e_3)=(1,\mathbf i,\mathbf j,\mathbf k)\).
This is Proposition 7 and Lemma 6 of \cite{frenkellibine} in our
normal notation.

The kernel \(\mathcal K\) is both left and right regular away from
zero and has unit outward flux on every centered sphere:
\[
 \int_{|r|=\varepsilon}\mathcal K(r)\frac r\varepsilon\,dS_r=1.
\]
Indeed the integrand is the scalar \((2\pi^2\varepsilon^3)^{-1}\)
and the sphere has area \(2\pi^2\varepsilon^3\).
It is the fundamental kernel for the unnormalized operator \(D\);
the fundamental kernel for \(\overline\partial\) is
\(2\mathcal K\). The boundary formula still uses
\(\mathcal K\) with the normalization displayed here.

Apply \eqref{eq:q21-bilinear-stokes} on
\(\Omega\setminus\overline{B_\varepsilon(q)}\), with
\(B(p)=\mathcal K(p-q)\). The inward orientation on the removed
sphere contributes \(-A(q)\) in the limit, because \(A\) is
continuous and the flux is one. Thus every \(C^1\) field \(A\)
defined near \(\overline\Omega\) satisfies
\begin{equation}
 A(q)=\int_{\partial\Omega}\mathcal K(p-q)n(p)A(p)\,dS_p
       -2\int_\Omega\mathcal K(p-q)\overline\partial_pA(p)\,dV_p.
 \label{eq:q21-borel-pompeiu}
\end{equation}
The volume kernel behaves as \(|p-q|^{-3}\) and is locally
integrable in four dimensions. This argument asserts no differentiation
of that singular volume integral. For regular \(F\), the volume term
vanishes and gives the left Cauchy--Fueter formula
\begin{equation}
 F(t,q)=\int_{\partial\Omega}\mathcal K(p-q)n(p)F(t,p)\,dS_p.
 \label{eq:q21-cauchy-formula}
\end{equation}
Its factor order is kernel, normal, field, as in Theorem 9 of
\cite{frenkellibine}.

For \(q\) in a compact interior set, define
\[
 \mathcal K_m(p;q)=
     \bigl(\partial_{q_0}^m\mathcal K(p-q)\bigr)n(p),
 \qquad
 A_m(t,q)=\int_{\partial\Omega}\mathcal K_m(p;q)F(t,p)\,dS_p.
\]
The boundary kernel is smooth and uniformly separated from its pole,
so differentiating \eqref{eq:q21-cauchy-formula} gives \(A_m=G_m\).
For \(q=x\in\mathbb R\),
\eqref{eq:q21-signed-projection} becomes the exact signed boundary
correlation
\begin{align}
 \Lag_\ell(t,x)
 &=\operatorname{Re}\bigl(\mathbf j\overline{A_\ell(t,x)}A_{\ell+1}(t,x)\bigr)
 \label{eq:q21-boundary-laguerre}\\
 &=\operatorname{Re}\left[
 \mathbf j\iint_{\partial\Omega\times\partial\Omega}
 \overline{F(t,p)}\,\overline{\mathcal K_\ell(p;x)}
 \mathcal K_{\ell+1}(r;x)F(t,r)\,dS_p\,dS_r\right].
 \nonumber
\end{align}
Quaternion conjugation reverses the order of the first kernel-field
product. This is a signed quadratic observation, not a positive square.

To make the first three observations explicit, take a ball of radius
\(R\) centered at a real \(x\), and write
\[
 B(\omega)=F(t,x+R\omega),\qquad
 \langle B\rangle=\frac1{2\pi^2}\int_{S^3}B(\omega)\,dS_\omega.
\]
Regularity implies harmonicity by \eqref{eq:q21-factorization}.
The componentwise harmonic Poisson formula is
\begin{equation}
 F(t,x+Ry)=\left\langle
 \frac{1-|y|^2}{|\omega-y|^4}B(\omega)\right\rangle,
 \qquad |y|<1.
 \label{eq:q21-poisson-formula}
\end{equation}
This is the dimension-four formula in Theorem 34 of
\cite{frenkellibine}. At \(y=0\), its kernel and its first two
\(y_0\) derivatives are respectively
\(1\), \(4\omega_0\), and \(24\omega_0^2-6\).
Thus, differentiating with the boundary sphere fixed,
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{
 A_0=\langle B\rangle,
 \qquad A_1=\frac4R\langle\omega_0B\rangle,
 \qquad A_2=\frac6{R^2}\langle(4\omega_0^2-1)B\rangle.}
 \label{eq:q21-sphere-moments}
\end{equation}
The \(R^{-1}\) and \(R^{-2}\) factors come from
\(q_0=x+Ry_0\). These are harmonic moment formulas, not pointwise
equalities between differentiated Cauchy and Poisson kernels.
For example, differentiating the Cauchy kernel directly at the center
gives \(R^{-1}\langle(4\omega_0-\omega)B\rangle\), which agrees
with \eqref{eq:q21-sphere-moments} because
\(\langle\omega B\rangle=0\) by the regular Stokes theorem.

Stationarity of \(H\) or \(H_x\) is respectively
\(P_0A_1=0\) or \(P_0A_2=0\). The physical energy is
\(|A_0|^2\), and the two Laguerre observations follow from
\eqref{eq:q21-boundary-laguerre}. For
\(M=\sup_{\partial B_R}|F|\), the moment formulas give
\[
 |A_1|\le4M/R,\qquad |A_2|\le18M/R^2.
\]
The constants use \(|\omega_0|\le1\) and
\(|6(4\omega_0^2-1)|\le18\). These upper estimates do not bound
the signed correlations from below through a cancellation of \(A_0\).
Moreover, the boundary trace samples complex heat times
\(\tau=t+\bar z_1\), so physical real-time signs are not automatically
signs of the boundary data.

\section{Currents, harmonic norms, orientation, and reciprocal controls}
\label{sec:q21-currents}

For each regular jet \(G_\ell\), define the pure-imaginary-quaternion
current
\[
 J_\mu^{(\ell)}=\operatorname{Im}_{\mathbb H}
       (\overline{G_\ell}\,\partial_{q_\mu}G_\ell),
 \qquad \operatorname{Im}_{\mathbb H}Q=Q-\operatorname{Re}Q.
\]
Since \(\Delta_4G_\ell=0\), the product rule gives
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{\mu=0}^3\partial_{q_\mu}J_\mu^{(\ell)}
 =\operatorname{Im}_{\mathbb H}\left(
   \sum_\mu|\partial_{q_\mu}G_\ell|^2+\overline{G_\ell}\Delta_4G_\ell\right)=0.
 \label{eq:q21-current-conservation}
\end{equation}
At real physical points,
\([J_0^{(\ell)}]_{\mathbf j}=-\Lag_\ell\) by
\eqref{eq:q21-laguerre-product}. Thus a Laguerre sign is an oriented
component of a conserved current. Zero closed-boundary flux does not
determine its sign at a point.

For the adopted lift, put \(e=q_3=-d\). Then
\(z_0=a+\mathbf ib\) and \(\tau=t+c+\mathbf ie\).
Differentiation of complex coefficients, with their placement retained,
gives
\[
 F_a=G_1,\quad F_b=G_1\mathbf i,
 \qquad F_c=-G_2,\quad F_e=-G_2\mathbf i.
\]
For instance \(\partial_b(U+\mathbf jV)
=\mathbf iU_{z_0}+\mathbf j\mathbf iV_{z_0}
=(U_{z_0}+\mathbf jV_{z_0})\mathbf i\).
Consequently harmonicity yields
\begin{equation}
 \Delta_4|F|^2
 =2\sum_\mu|\partial_{q_\mu}F|^2
 =4\bigl(|h_1|^2+2|h_2|^2+|h_3|^2\bigr).
 \label{eq:q21-norm-laplacian}
\end{equation}

There is a further logarithmic identity special to the holomorphic pair
\((u,v)=(H,H_z)\) in \((z_0,\tau)\). Wherever
\(E=|u|^2+|v|^2>0\), direct complex differentiation gives, for
\(\alpha=z_0\) or \(\tau\),
\[
 \partial_\alpha\partial_{\bar\alpha}\log E
 =\frac{E(|u_\alpha|^2+|v_\alpha|^2)
       -|\bar uu_\alpha+\bar vv_\alpha|^2}{E^2}
 =\frac{|uv_\alpha-vu_\alpha|^2}{E^2}.
\]
The two determinants are
\(uv_{z_0}-vu_{z_0}=-\Lag_0\) and
\(uv_\tau-vu_\tau=h_1h_2-h_0h_3=\partial_z\Lag_0\).
Since
\(\Delta_4=4(\partial_{z_0}\partial_{\bar z_0}
 +\partial_\tau\partial_{\bar\tau})\) at fixed external time,
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\Delta_4\log|F|^2
 =\frac{4\bigl(|\Lag_0|^2+
       |\partial_z\Lag_0|^2\bigr)}{|F|^4}.}
 \label{eq:q21-lognorm-laplacian}
\end{equation}
This statement is restricted to \(F\ne0\) and uses the declared
two-variable holomorphic pair, beyond general Fueter regularity.
Both norm identities erase the orientation by taking squares;
subharmonicity of a norm or logarithmic norm does not exclude zeros.

The real Jacobian has a fixed orientation sign. With domain coordinates
\((a,b,c,e)\), the pair \((z_0,\tau)=(a+\mathbf ib,t+c+\mathbf ie)\)
has the usual complex orientation. Its complex derivative matrix is
\[
 \begin{pmatrix}h_1&-h_2\\h_2&-h_3\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad\det_{\mathbb C}=h_2^2-h_1h_3=\Lag_1.
\]
The real determinant of a complex-linear map is the squared modulus of
its complex determinant. But the quaternion output coordinates of
\(u+\mathbf jv\) are
\((\operatorname{Re}u,\operatorname{Im}u,
  \operatorname{Re}v,-\operatorname{Im}v)\), reversing one coordinate.
Therefore
\begin{equation}
 \det_{\mathbb R}(d_qF)=-|\Lag_1(\tau,z_0)|^2.
 \label{eq:q21-jacobian}
\end{equation}
The complex determinant in the opposite variable order \((\tau,z_0)\)
is \(-\Lag_1\). At a double physical zero,
\(h_0=h_1=0\) and \(h_2\ne0\), so
\(\Lag_1=h_2^2\ne0\). Such a scalar collision is a
nondegenerate zero of the quaternion map, not a forbidden degeneracy.

Conjugating the left equation gives
\[
 \overline F\,D^-:=
 \partial_{q_0}\overline F
 -\partial_{q_1}\overline F\,\mathbf i
 -\partial_{q_2}\overline F\,\mathbf j
 -\partial_{q_3}\overline F\,\mathbf k=0.
\]
Thus \(\overline F\) is right regular for the conjugate operator;
it is not generally right regular for \(D\).
In \eqref{eq:q21-bilinear-stokes}, substituting
\(B=\overline F,A=F\) leaves the volume term
\((\overline F D)F\). Dropping it would not be justified.

Pointwise reciprocal and logarithmic derivative arguments likewise
require care. The entire backward-heat solution \(H(\tau,z)=z\)
has the regular, nowhere-zero lift \(F=z_0+\mathbf j\), but
\begin{equation}
 F^{-1}=\frac{\bar z_0-\mathbf j}{1+|z_0|^2},\qquad
 \overline\partial_q(F^{-1})
 =\frac{1+\mathbf j\bar z_0}{(1+|z_0|^2)^2}\ne0.
 \label{eq:q21-inverse-control}
\end{equation}
For this calculation, write
\(U=\bar z_0/(1+|z_0|^2)\),
\(V=-1/(1+|z_0|^2)\) in
\eqref{eq:q21-component-fueter}; then
\(U_{\bar z_0}=(1+|z_0|^2)^{-2}\) and
\(V_{z_0}=\bar z_0(1+|z_0|^2)^{-2}\).
Here \(F^{-1}\partial_qF=F^{-1}\) is also nonregular.
The general failure of ordinary products and compositions to preserve
Fueter regularity is discussed in \cite{sudbery}; the explicit
control supplies the reciprocal obstruction needed here.

On the real physical slice only, whenever \(F\ne0\),
\begin{equation}
 F^{-1}F_x
 =\tfrac12\partial_x\log\mathcal E
       -\mathbf j\frac{\Lag_0}{\mathcal E}.
 \label{eq:q21-physical-logderivative}
\end{equation}
This is an algebraic identity obtained by dividing
\eqref{eq:q21-laguerre-product} by \(\mathcal E\); it asserts no
ambient regularity of the quotient. Locally writing
\(F=\sqrt{\mathcal E}\,e^{\mathbf j\theta}\) identifies its
\(\mathbf j\) component with the phase derivative
\(\theta_x=-\Lag_0/\mathcal E\).

Finally, the backward-heat polynomial
\(P(\tau,z)=z^2-2\tau+1\) gives
\[
 F_P=z_0^2-2t-2\bar z_1+1+2\mathbf jz_0,
 \qquad
 A_0=x^2-2t+1+2\mathbf jx,
 \quad A_1=2x+2\mathbf j,
 \quad A_2=2.
\]
Equations \eqref{eq:q21-signed-projection} and
\eqref{eq:q21-jacobian} give
\(\Lag_0=2x^2+4t-2\), \(\Lag_1=4\), and
\(\det d_qF_P=-16\).
The physical field nevertheless vanishes at \((t,x)=(1/2,0)\).
This control satisfies the lift, regularity, heat, and boundary identities,
but it violates the first stationary sign before its collision.
The equations therefore organize the RH observation without replacing
the needed estimate from the complete theta source.

\section{A complete theta estimate on a bounded spectral domain}
\label{sec:theta-central}

For clarity, all source variables in this section are denoted by \(u\).
The physical spectral coordinate is \(x\), and \(t\) is independent
real Newman time.  We use the normalization
\begin{align}
 \phi_n(u)&=\bigl(2\pi^2n^4e^{9u}-3\pi n^2e^{5u}\bigr)
                 e^{-\pi n^2e^{4u}}, \qquad u\geq0,\quad n\geq1,
 \label{eq:theta-summands}\\
 \Phi(u)&=\sum_{n\geq1}\phi_n(u),\qquad
 H(t,x)=\int_0^\infty e^{tu^2}\Phi(u)\cos(xu)\,du .
 \label{eq:theta-halfline}
\end{align}
Thus \(H(0,z)=\xi(1/2+iz/2)/8\).  The complete source extends to an
even smooth function on the real line.  In particular, every odd
endpoint derivative of \(\Phi\) vanishes.  One direct way to see this
is to put
\[
 h(u)=e^u\Theta(e^{4u},0),\qquad
 \Theta(r,y)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}e^{-\pi n^2r+2\pi iny}.
\]
Jacobi inversion gives \(h(-u)=h(u)\), and differentiation of its
normally convergent series gives
\(\Phi=(h''-h)/16\) on \(u\geq0\).
Every fixed source derivative has superexponential decay at infinity.
Consequently the cosine transform in \eqref{eq:theta-halfline} is
entire in \((t,x)\), differentiation under its integral is legitimate,
and its real spectral restriction is Schwartz for each fixed real \(t\).
No finite theta truncation is made here.

For \(0\leq t\leq1\), define
\[
 w_t(u)=e^{tu^2}\Phi(u),\qquad
 M_j(t)=\int_0^\infty u^jw_t(u)\,du,\qquad
 \delta=\frac1{120000}.
\]
Every \(\phi_n(u)\) is strictly positive on this half-line:
writing \(q=\pi n^2e^{4u}\), its factor \(2q-3\) is positive.

\begin{theorem}[Complete-source central moment estimate]
\label{thm:central-moment}
For every \(0\leq t\leq1\),
\begin{equation}
 M_2(t)-\frac{25}{2}M_4(t)>
 \frac{2413193}{268912000000}>\delta .
 \label{eq:central-moment}
\end{equation}
Both the entire theta-index sum and the negative integration tail are
included in this estimate.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
First consider \(0\leq u\leq1/10\).  With \(q=\pi e^{4u}\),
\[
 \phi_1(u)=e^u g(q),\qquad
 g(q)=q(2q-3)e^{-q},\qquad
 g'(q)=-(2q-1)(q-3)e^{-q}.
\]
The elementary bounds \(\pi<22/7\) and \(e^{2/5}<3/2\) give
\(3<q<33/7\).  Hence \(g\) is decreasing on the required interval,
and \(e<11/4\), \(e^{2/7}>9/7\) give
\begin{equation}
 \Phi(u)\geq\phi_1(u)>g(33/7)
 >\frac{1485}{49}\frac97\left(\frac4{11}\right)^5
 >\frac6{25}.
 \label{eq:theta-core}
\end{equation}
Only positive summands have been discarded.

For the complete upper tail let \(y=\pi e^{4u}\geq\pi\).
The ratio between successive terms of \(n^4e^{-yn^2}\) is at most
\[
 16e^{-3y}\leq16e^{-3\pi}<\frac12.
\]
Dropping the negative part of each summand in
\eqref{eq:theta-summands} and summing its full geometric majorant yields
\begin{equation}
 \Phi(u)<4\pi^2e^{9u-\pi e^{4u}},\qquad u\geq0.
 \label{eq:complete-theta-majorant}
\end{equation}
Set \(b=7/25\) and \(L_t(u)=9u+tu^2-\pi e^{4u}\).
Using \(\pi>25/8\) and \(e^{28/25}>153/50\), one obtains
\[
 L_t(b)<\frac{1624}{625}-\frac{153}{16}<-\frac{69}{10},
 \qquad
 L_t'(b)<9+\frac{14}{25}-\frac{153}{4}<-28.
\]
Moreover \(L_t''(u)=2t-16\pi e^{4u}<0\) for \(u\geq b\).
Thus \(L_t(u)<-69/10-28(u-b)\) on the full tail.
Finally \(4\pi^2<40\) and
\[
 e^{69/10}>
 \left(\frac83\right)^6
 \left(1+\frac9{10}+\frac12\left(\frac9{10}\right)^2\right)>800
\]
prove the uniform envelope
\begin{equation}
 w_t(u)<\frac1{20}e^{-28(u-b)},\qquad u\geq b.
 \label{eq:heated-tail}
\end{equation}

On \([0,b]\), the factor \(u^2(1-25u^2/2)\) is nonnegative.
On \(u\geq b\) it is bounded below by
\(-25u^2(u^2-b^2)/2\), because \(1-25b^2/2=1/50\).
Apply \eqref{eq:theta-core} to the positive core,
discard the nonnegative remainder in \([0,b]\), and pay the whole
negative tail using \eqref{eq:heated-tail}.  This gives
\[
 M_2-\frac{25}{2}M_4>
 \frac6{25}\int_0^{1/10}u^2(1-25u^2/2)\,du-\frac58 I,
\]
where, after the substitution \(u=b+s\),
\begin{align*}
 I&=\int_b^\infty u^2(u^2-b^2)e^{-28(u-b)}\,du\\
  &=\frac{2b^3}{28^2}+\frac{10b^2}{28^3}
    +\frac{24b}{28^4}+\frac{24}{28^5}
    =\frac{3497259}{33614000000}.
\end{align*}
The retained difference is exactly
\[
 \frac{37}{500000}-\frac58 I
 =\frac{2413193}{268912000000}>\frac1{120000}.
\]
This proves \eqref{eq:central-moment}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Physical norm and stationary signs on the central domain]
\label{cor:central-gap}
For \(0\leq t\leq1\),
\begin{align}
 H_{xx}(t,x)&<-\delta &&(|x|\leq5), \label{eq:central-curvature}\\
 -H_x(t,x)&>x\delta &&(0<x\leq8), \label{eq:central-slope}\\
 H(t,x)&>\frac{7733}{350000}>\frac1{50} &&(|x|\leq1), \label{eq:central-value}\\
 |H(t,x)+\mathbf jH_x(t,x)|&>\delta &&(|x|\leq8).
 \label{eq:central-physical-gap}
\end{align}
On \(|x|\leq5\), the only first stationary point is \(x=0\), and the
second stationary set is empty.  At that first stationary point,
\begin{equation}
 \mathscr L_0(t,0)=H(t,0)M_2(t)>\frac1{5000000}.
 \label{eq:central-stationary}
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
For every real \(y\), \(\cos y\geq1-y^2/2\).
Integrating this inequality from zero gives
\(\sin y\geq y-y^3/6\) for every \(y\geq0\).
Because \(w_t\) is positive, these global inequalities imply
\[
 -H_{xx}\geq M_2-\frac{x^2}{2}M_4,\qquad
 -H_x\geq x\left(M_2-\frac{x^2}{6}M_4\right)\quad(x>0).
\]
The first right side exceeds \(\delta\) for \(|x|\leq5\).
The second exceeds \(x\delta\) for \(0<x\leq8\), since
\(8^2/6<25/2\).  Evenness handles negative \(x\).
These estimates keep the oscillatory source tail rather than replacing
its cosine or sine by a presumed sign.

For \(|x|\leq1\), the core satisfies
\(\cos(xu)\geq199/200\) on \(0\leq u\leq1/10\).
The interval \(1/10\leq u\leq b\) has positive cosine.
The remaining tail costs at most \(1/560\), by
\eqref{eq:heated-tail}.  Therefore
\[
 H(t,x)>\frac6{25}\frac1{10}\frac{199}{200}-\frac1{560}
 =\frac{7733}{350000}>\frac1{50}.
\]
Use this scalar component for \(|x|\leq1\), and
\eqref{eq:central-slope} for \(1\leq|x|\leq8\), to prove
\eqref{eq:central-physical-gap}.
Strict concavity and evenness give the assertion about stationary sets.
Finally \(H(t,0)>3/125\) follows by integrating the positive core alone,
and \(M_2>\delta\) follows from \eqref{eq:central-moment}.
Their product is greater than \(1/5000000\).
\end{proof}

The bounded range \(|x|\leq8\) is part of the theorem.
It does not exclude collisions at unknown spectral heights.

\section{Full angular gluing and absolute observation stability}
\label{sec:gluing-stability}

Let \((c_n)_{n\in\mathbb Z}\) be a bounded real sequence satisfying
\(c_{-n}=c_n\) and \(c_0=1\).  Its prepared theta field is
\[
 \Theta_c(r,y)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}c_ne^{-\pi n^2r+2\pi iny},
 \qquad r>0.
\]
Boundedness guarantees normal convergence on compact angular sets.
The complete imaginary elliptic defect is
\begin{equation}
 E_c(r,y)=e^{-\pi r}
 \left[\Theta_c(r,y+ir)-e^{\pi r-2\pi iy}\Theta_c(r,y)\right].
 \label{eq:elliptic-defect}
\end{equation}
We use its full angular norm at the \emph{single} scale \(r=1\).
This is an infinite Fourier observation, not a finite jet condition or
an estimate over all theta scales.

\begin{proposition}[Exact defect norm and balanced gauge]
\label{prop:defect-parseval}
Put \(d_m=c_m-c_{m-1}\) for \(m\geq1\) and
\(w_m=e^{-2\pi m^2}+e^{-2\pi(m-1)^2}\).  Then
\begin{equation}
 D^2:=\int_0^1|E_c(1,y)|^2\,dy
 =\sum_{m\geq1}|d_m|^2w_m.
 \label{eq:defect-parseval}
\end{equation}
For \(\mathcal B_c(0,z)=e^{\pi z^2/2}\Theta_c(1,z)\),
\begin{equation}
 D^2=\int_0^1 e^{-\pi(1+y^2)}
 \left|\mathcal B_c(0,y+i)
 -e^{\pi/2-\pi iy}\mathcal B_c(0,y)\right|^2dy.
 \label{eq:defect-balanced}
\end{equation}
In particular \(D=0\) forces \(c_n=1\) for every \(n\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Termwise substitution into \eqref{eq:elliptic-defect}, followed by the
index change in the second term, gives the normally convergent series
\[
 E_c(r,y)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}(c_n-c_{n+1})
 e^{-\pi(n+1)^2r}e^{2\pi iny}.
\]
Parseval on \([0,1]\) gives its squared coefficient norm.
For \(n=m-1\geq0\), the coefficient difference is \(-d_m\) and its
weight is \(e^{-2\pi m^2}\).  For \(n=-m\leq-1\), symmetry gives
the difference \(d_m\) and the weight \(e^{-2\pi(m-1)^2}\).
Their sum proves \eqref{eq:defect-parseval}.
Every weight is positive; \(c_0=1\) proves the rigidity statement.

For real \(y\), direct multiplication of the balanced gauge gives
\[
 E_c(1,y)=e^{-\pi-\pi(y+i)^2/2}
 \left[\mathcal B_c(0,y+i)
 -e^{\pi/2-\pi iy}\mathcal B_c(0,y)\right].
\]
The squared modulus of the prefactor is \(e^{-\pi(1+y^2)}\), proving
\eqref{eq:defect-balanced}.
\end{proof}

The stability observation below is explicitly defined on the half-line:
\begin{equation}
 \Phi_c^+(u)=\sum_{n\geq1}c_n\phi_n(u),\qquad
 H_c(t,x)=\int_0^\infty e^{tu^2}\Phi_c^+(u)\cos(xu)\,du.
 \label{eq:perturbed-halfline}
\end{equation}
For general \(c\), its even source extension need not be smooth at
zero or equal the projection of the full modular prepared field.
It is nonetheless entire in \((t,x)\), obeys backward heat, and is real
and even in real \(x\).  If all \(c_n>0\), its source is positive.

\begin{theorem}[Four-jet gluing stability]
\label{thm:four-jet-gluing}
For every real \(x\), \(0\leq t\leq1\), and \(j=0,1,2,3\),
\begin{equation}
 |\partial_x^j(H_c-H)(t,x)|\leq DC_j,\qquad
 C_j^2=\sum_{m\geq1}\frac{A_{jm}^2}{w_m},
 \label{eq:four-jet-stability}
\end{equation}
where the full positive sums are
\[
 A_{jm}=\sum_{n\geq m}
 \frac{2\pi^2n^4j!e^{-\pi n^2}}{(4\pi n^2-9)^{j+1}}.
\]
These finite constants satisfy
\begin{equation}
 C_0<\frac12,\qquad C_1<\frac18,\qquad
 C_2<\frac1{12},\qquad C_3<\frac1{12}.
 \label{eq:four-jet-rational}
\end{equation}
The same four rational constants bound the genuine jets
\(|\partial_x^jH|\) on this domain.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let \(\Delta h_j=\partial_x^j(H_c-H)\).
Using \(c_n-1=\sum_{m=1}^n d_m\), the complete series can be regrouped as
\[
 \Delta h_j=\sum_{m\geq1}d_m B_{jm},\qquad
 B_{jm}=\sum_{n\geq m}\int_0^\infty
 e^{tu^2}\phi_n(u)u^j\cos(xu+j\pi/2)\,du.
\]
Absolute convergence justifies this regrouping: the coefficient
sequence is bounded, so \(\sum_{m\leq n}|d_m|=O(n)\), while the
moment bounds below decay as \(e^{-\pi n^2}\).
For \(u\geq0\), \(e^{4u}\geq1+4u+8u^2\).
Drop the negative summand in \(\phi_n\), and note \(t-8\pi n^2<0\):
\[
 e^{tu^2}\phi_n(u)
 \leq2\pi^2n^4e^{-\pi n^2}
 e^{-(4\pi n^2-9)u}.
\]
Integrating \(u^j\) against this full half-line envelope proves
\(|B_{jm}|\leq A_{jm}\).  Cauchy--Schwarz against the exact
weights in \eqref{eq:defect-parseval} proves
\eqref{eq:four-jet-stability}.

To bound the constants, set
\[
 a_j=\frac{2\pi^2j!}{(4\pi-9)^{j+1}},\quad
 \rho_0=4e^{-3\pi},\quad
 \rho_j=e^{-3\pi}\ (j\geq1),\quad r_*=e^{-4\pi}.
\]
Since \(4\pi n^2-9\geq(4\pi-9)n^2\), the summand in \(A_{jm}\)
is at most \(a_jn^{2-2j}e^{-\pi n^2}\).
Successive ratios of this majorant are bounded by \(\rho_j\), giving
\[
 A_{jm}\leq\frac{a_jm^{2-2j}e^{-\pi m^2}}{1-\rho_j}.
\]
Because \(w_m\geq e^{-2\pi(m-1)^2}\), this implies
\begin{align*}
 C_0^2&\leq
 \left(\frac{a_0e^{-\pi}}{1-\rho_0}\right)^2
 \frac{1+11r_*+11r_*^2+r_*^3}{(1-r_*)^5},\\
 C_j^2&\leq
 \left(\frac{a_je^{-\pi}}{1-\rho_j}\right)^2
 \frac1{1-r_*},\qquad j=1,2,3.
\end{align*}
Here the first identity uses
\(\sum_{m\geq1}m^4r^{m-1}=(1+11r+11r^2+r^3)/(1-r)^5\);
for \(j\geq1\), \(m^{4-4j}\leq1\).
The elementary bounds
\[
 3<\pi<22/7,\quad e^3>20,\quad
 e^{-\pi}<1/20,\quad
 \rho_0<1/2000,\quad \rho_j<1/8000,\quad r_*<1/160000
\]
reduce the asserted strict rational bounds to finite rational
comparisons, detailed in the reproducibility appendix.
Finally the genuine moment bound is \(|\partial_x^jH|\leq A_{j1}\);
the same majorants give its stated rational bounds.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Absolute signed-form stability and a robust central gap]
\label{cor:robust-gluing}
Let \(\mathscr L_j(f)=(f^{(j+1)})^2-f^{(j)}f^{(j+2)}\).
On every real \(x\) and \(0\leq t\leq1\),
\begin{align}
 |\mathscr L_0(H_c)-\mathscr L_0(H)|
 &\leq\frac{11}{192}(2D+D^2), \label{eq:L0-defect-transfer}\\
 |\mathscr L_1(H_c)-\mathscr L_1(H)|
 &\leq\frac5{288}(2D+D^2). \label{eq:L1-defect-transfer}
\end{align}
If \(D\leq1/30000\), then
\begin{equation}
 |H_c(t,x)+\mathbf jH_{c,x}(t,x)|>
 \frac1{240000},\qquad |x|\leq8,\quad 0\leq t\leq1.
 \label{eq:robust-central-gap}
\end{equation}
On \(|x|\leq5\), \(H_{c,xx}<-1/180000\); the only first stationary
point is zero, the second stationary set is empty, and
\(\mathscr L_0(H_c;t,0)>1/9000000\).
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Write \(S_0=1/2,S_1=1/8,S_2=S_3=1/12\).
Expansion of the quadratic Laguerre form, followed by
\(|h_j|<S_j\) and \(|\Delta h_j|\leq DS_j\), bounds its error by
\[
 (S_{j+1}^2+S_jS_{j+2})(2D+D^2).
\]
The two coefficients are \(11/192\) and \(5/288\).
This comparison is at the same spectral point; it does not assume
that stationary sets of \(H\) and \(H_c\) coincide.

For the norm bound, on \(|x|\leq1\) the scalar component satisfies
\[
 H_c>1/50-D/2>1/100.
\]
On \(1\leq|x|\leq8\), the derivative component satisfies
\[
 |H_{c,x}|>\delta-D/8\geq1/240000.
\]
The curvature transfer gives
\(H_{c,xx}<-\delta+D/12\leq-1/180000\).
Evenness now identifies the stationary sets.
At zero \(H_c>3/125-D/2>1/50\), and multiplication of the last
curvature gap by \(1/50\) proves the stated Laguerre margin.
\end{proof}

The estimates \eqref{eq:L0-defect-transfer}--\eqref{eq:L1-defect-transfer}
transfer a separately established margin.  They do not supply the
genuine theta sign, or a loss bounded relative to \(H^2+H_x^2\).

\section{The scope of an approximate gluing certificate}
\label{sec:remote-control}

\begin{proposition}[Arbitrarily small defects with remote joint zeros]
\label{prop:remote-gluing-controls}
Fix \(T\in(0,1)\).  There are bounded positive symmetric coefficient
sequences with \(c_0=1\), defects \(D_X>0\) tending to zero, and
half-line observations \eqref{eq:perturbed-halfline} satisfying
\[
 H_c(T,X)=H_{c,x}(T,X)=0
\]
at an unbounded sequence of real heights \(X\).
The coefficient changes and \(D_X\) decrease faster than every
inverse power of \(X\).  These observations fail exact imaginary
gluing and smooth modular reflection; they are not genuine theta
counterexamples.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Put \(K_n(x)=\int_0^\infty e^{Tu^2}\phi_n(u)\cos(xu)\,du\),
and \(\alpha_n=\pi n^2\).
The polynomial recurrence
\[
 P_0(q)=2q-3,\qquad
 P_{k+1}(q)=(5-4q)P_k(q)+4qP_k'(q)
\]
gives
\(\phi_n^{(k)}(0)=\alpha_ne^{-\alpha_n}P_k(\alpha_n)\).
In particular,
\[
 P_1(q)=-8q^2+30q-15,\quad
 P_3(q)=-128q^4+1440q^3-4232q^2+3270q-375.
\]
Write \(b_n=\phi_n'(0)\) and \(e_n=\phi_n'''(0)\).
Repeated integration by parts, with all derivatives integrable, gives
\[
 K_n=-b_nx^{-2}+(e_n+6Tb_n)x^{-4}+O(x^{-6}),
\]
\[
 K_n'=2b_nx^{-3}-4(e_n+6Tb_n)x^{-5}+O(x^{-7}).
\]
The derivative expansion can be obtained independently from its sine
integral, so no differentiation of an unspecified remainder is needed.
Consequently
\[
 \det\begin{pmatrix}K_1&K_2\\K_1'&K_2'\end{pmatrix}
 =8\pi^2e^{-5\pi}P(\pi)x^{-7}+O(x^{-9}),
\]
where
\[
 P(q)=-113400q+907200q^2-2570400q^3
       +3081600q^4-1520640q^5+245760q^6.
\]
This polynomial is \(P_1(q)P_3(4q)-P_1(4q)P_3(q)\).
Exact rational interval evaluation gives
\(4.598266<P(\pi)<4.598268\), as detailed in the appendix.
The matrix is therefore invertible for every sufficiently large \(x\),
and its inverse grows at most polynomially.

Choose an unbounded sequence of sufficiently large \(X\) with
\(H(T,X)\ne0\), possible because \(H(T,\cdot)\) is nonzero entire.
Solve
\[
 \begin{pmatrix}K_1(X)&K_2(X)\\K_1'(X)&K_2'(X)\end{pmatrix}
 \binom{a_X}{b_X}
 =-\binom{H(T,X)}{H_x(T,X)} .
\]
The right side is Schwartz, so \(a_X,b_X\) decrease faster than every
inverse power of \(X\).  Let \(c_1=1+a_X,c_2=1+b_X\), retain
\(c_n=1\) for \(n\geq3\), and impose symmetry and \(c_0=1\).
For large \(X\) all coefficients are positive and the defining system
gives the exact joint zero.  The coefficient change is nonzero, and
\[
 D_X^2=a_X^2w_1+(b_X-a_X)^2w_2+b_X^2w_3>0
\]
tends to zero at the same superalgebraic rate.
The unheated odd-jet matrix
\(\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}b_1&b_2\\e_1&e_2\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\)
has determinant \(4\pi^2e^{-5\pi}P(\pi)\ne0\).
Thus the nonzero perturbation cannot leave both the first and third
odd endpoint jets zero.  Its even extension is not smooth, and cannot
be a smooth modular projection.
\end{proof}

\section{Signed paired kernels and endpoint compensation}
\label{sec:paired-compensation}

For real \(t,x\), put \(g_{n,t}(u)=e^{tu^2}\phi_n(u)\).
Direct expansion of the physical Laguerre forms gives the exact
complete paired identity
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
 \mathscr L_j(t,x)=\frac14\sum_{n,m\geq1}
 \iint_{(0,\infty)^2}
 &g_{n,t}(u)g_{m,t}(v)(uv)^j\\
 {}\times\bigl[&(u+v)^2\cos x(u-v)
 +(-1)^j(u-v)^2\cos x(u+v)\bigr]\,du\,dv,
 \qquad j=0,1 .
\end{split}
\label{eq:complete-paired-kernel}
\end{equation}
Absolute convergence follows from the complete theta source moments
through order four.  To derive it, write
\(H^{(j)}=\sum_n\int u^jg_{n,t}(u)\cos(xu+j\pi/2)\,du\),
symmetrize \(H^{(j)}H^{(j+2)}\) in \(u,v\), and use the
product-to-sum identities.  Both terms, and all off-diagonal
indices \(n\ne m\), remain.  Substitution of the same source into
Fueter boundary reproduction produces this identity: the boundary
radius disappears under exact reproduction, rather than creating a
new positive multiplier.

The individual scalar channel \(H_{n,t}=\int g_{n,t}\cos(xu)\,du\)
has an algebraic endpoint tail:
\[
 H_{n,t}(x)=-b_nx^{-2}+O(x^{-4}),
 \qquad b_n=\phi_n'(0).
\]
Repeated endpoint expansions of its derivatives give, when \(b_n\ne0\),
\begin{equation}
 \mathscr L_j(H_{n,t};x)=
 -(j+2)((j+1)!)^2b_n^2x^{-2j-6}
 +O(x^{-2j-8}),\qquad j=0,1 .
 \label{eq:individual-negative-tail}
\end{equation}
Indeed the leading coefficient is
\((j+2)!^2-(j+1)!(j+3)!=-(j+2)((j+1)!)^2\).
Thus separate diagonal channels are eventually negative.
For the complete source, in contrast,
\[
 \sum_{n\geq1}\phi_n^{(2k+1)}(0)=0,\qquad k\geq0.
\]
The full heated source has the same cancellation of every odd
endpoint jet, because \(e^{tu^2}\) is even.
This is necessary compensation for its Schwartz Fourier decay.
It rules out a global proof that treats individual diagonal channels
as nonnegative or ignores endpoint cancellation in a theta truncation.

\begin{proposition}[Compensated all-index truncation]
\label{prop:compensated-tails}
Fix a positive integer \(K\).  There exist odd Schwartz functions
\(\beta_0,\ldots,\beta_{K-1}\) on \(\mathbb R\), each an odd
polynomial times \(e^{-u^2}\), satisfying
\[
 \beta_k^{(2\ell+1)}(0)=\delta_{k\ell},\qquad 0\leq k,\ell<K.
\]
Define, on \(u\geq0\),
\[
 \widetilde g_{n,t}(u)=g_{n,t}(u)
 -\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}g_{n,t}^{(2k+1)}(0)\beta_k(u),
 \qquad
 R_N(u)=\sum_{n>N}\widetilde g_{n,t}(u).
\]
Then
\(\sum_{n\geq1}\widetilde g_{n,t}=e^{tu^2}\Phi\), and, for
\(j=0,1,2,3\), \(x\ne0\),
\begin{equation}
 \left|\int_0^\infty u^jR_N(u)\cos(xu+j\pi/2)\,du\right|
 \leq |x|^{-2K}\left\|(u^jR_N)^{(2K)}\right\|_{L^1(0,\infty)}.
 \label{eq:compensated-ibp}
\end{equation}
The norm on the right includes every omitted index and is finite.
The following explicit majorant gives an all-index payment for it.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The jets of \(u^{2k+1}e^{-u^2}\) form a triangular matrix, with
nonzero diagonal, so finite triangular interpolation constructs
the \(\beta_k\).  For example \(\beta_0=ue^{-u^2}\) when \(K=1\).
Every compensated summand has its first \(K\) odd jets zero.
Absolute convergence of the endpoint-jet sums and full-source
cancellation show that their complete sum is unchanged.
For even \(j\), the cosine integral in \eqref{eq:compensated-ibp}
has boundary terms involving odd derivatives of \(u^jR_N\);
for odd \(j\), its sine integral has boundary terms involving even
derivatives.  Each relevant derivative below order \(2K\) is zero,
because its surviving derivative of \(R_N\) is odd and below \(2K\).
All upper endpoints vanish.  Repeating integration by parts \(2K\)
times therefore proves \eqref{eq:compensated-ibp}.
\end{proof}

Here is an explicit positive-polynomial majorant for the remainder norm;
it avoids leaving a finite theta cutoff unpaid.
Put \(\alpha_n=\pi n^2\), \(q=\alpha_ne^{4u}\).
The derivative representation
\[
 g_{n,t}^{(k)}(u)=
 \alpha_ne^{tu^2+5u-q}P_{k,t}(u,q)
\]
has the exact recurrence
\[
 P_{0,t}=2q-3,\quad
 P_{k+1,t}=(2tu+5-4q)P_{k,t}
 +\partial_uP_{k,t}+4q\partial_qP_{k,t}.
\]
For \(0\leq t\leq1\), define nonnegative-coefficient polynomials
\[
 Q_0=2q+3,\quad
 Q_{k+1}=(2u+5+4q)Q_k+\partial_uQ_k+4q\partial_qQ_k.
\]
Coefficientwise absolute-value comparison in the polynomial variables
\(t,u,q\), followed by \(0\leq t\leq1\), proves
\(|P_{k,t}(u,q)|\leq Q_k(u,q)\) for \(u,q\geq0\).
Leibniz's rule gives the positive polynomial
\[
 W_{Kj}(u,q)=
 \sum_{\ell=0}^{\min(j,2K)}
 \binom{2K}{\ell}\frac{j!}{(j-\ell)!}
 u^{j-\ell}Q_{2K-\ell}(u,q)
 =\sum_{a,b}\omega_{ab}u^aq^b .
\]
All \(\omega_{ab}\) are nonnegative.  Also write
\(Q_{2k+1}(0,q)=\sum_b\eta_{kb}q^b\), and put
\[
 B_{Kjk}=\left\|(u^j\beta_k)^{(2K)}\right\|_{L^1(0,\infty)}.
\]
These are explicit finite Gaussian-polynomial integrals.  An elementary
upper bound is obtained by summing absolute polynomial coefficients
times
\(\int_0^\infty u^ae^{-u^2}\,du=\Gamma((a+1)/2)/2\).

Choose \(N\) so that
\(\alpha_{N+1}=\pi(N+1)^2\geq8K+9\).
For every integer \(s\) occurring below, define
\[
 T_s(N)=
 \frac{\alpha_{N+1}^{\,s}e^{-\alpha_{N+1}}}
 {1-e^{-\pi(N+2)}}.
\]
Then the complete all-index estimate is
\begin{equation}
 \left\|(u^jR_N)^{(2K)}\right\|_1
 \leq
 \sum_{a,b}\omega_{ab}\frac{a!}{3^{a+1}}T_{b-a}(N)
 +\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}B_{Kjk}\sum_b\eta_{kb}T_{b+1}(N).
 \label{eq:explicit-compensated-tail}
\end{equation}
To prove this, a monomial \(u^aq^b\) in the derivative bound contributes
\[
 \alpha_n^{b+1}\int_0^\infty
 u^ae^{u^2+(5+4b)u-\alpha_ne^{4u}}\,du
 \leq \frac{a!}{3^{a+1}}\alpha_n^{b-a}e^{-\alpha_n}.
\]
Here \(b\leq2K+1\), so \(5+4b\leq8K+9\leq\alpha_n\).
The inequality \(e^{4u}\geq1+4u+8u^2\) leaves a linear decay rate
at least \(3\alpha_n\) and a nonpositive quadratic exponent.
The endpoint coefficients obey
\[
 |g_{n,t}^{(2k+1)}(0)|
 \leq e^{-\alpha_n}\sum_b\eta_{kb}\alpha_n^{b+1}.
\]
Finally every positive exponent \(s\) in these bounds satisfies
\(s\leq2K+1\), hence \(2s\leq\alpha_n\).
For \(n\geq N+1\), the successive ratio of
\(\alpha_n^se^{-\alpha_n}\) is at most \(e^{-\pi(n+1)}\):
use \(\log(1+1/n)\leq1/n\) if \(s>0\);
for \(s\leq0\) the bound is even smaller.
Summing the whole geometric majorant gives
\(\sum_{n>N}\alpha_n^se^{-\alpha_n}\leq T_s(N)\).
Applying the triangle inequality to the compensated source proves
\eqref{eq:explicit-compensated-tail}.

Compensation is a decomposition at each fixed time.  Its separate
channels need not solve heat or be positive.  Finite \(K\) improves a
bounded-height certificate by paying known endpoint cancellations;
it still gives algebraic error, not a global error relative to the
potentially much smaller physical norm.


\section{What remains to prove}\label{sec:remaining}
The quaternion field is fixed by its theta trace, its jet constraint, and
its heat-time displacement. Its regularity and its Cauchy formula are
exact identities. The bounded central estimate proves nonvanishing on
its declared rectangle, and the gluing theorem quantitatively controls
errors in a declared coefficient class. Neither theorem supplies a
collision-excluding sign at every possible positive threshold height.

A sufficient next result would bound the two signed physical observations
on predecessor neighborhoods by a loss locally bounded through joint
zeros. Equivalently, one may control the time derivative of the physical
norm on the union of the two stationary sets. The boundary expressions
give the same signed observations as full theta-index pairings. Their
cross terms and endpoint cancellations must be retained. The scalar
whole-plane curvature criterion provides a separate exact route, with
the scalar projection of the quaternion trace as its observation.

These statements specify several equivalent or sufficient mathematical
targets. They do not promote Fueter regularity, a subharmonic quaternion
norm, or an absolute boundary derivative estimate to an arithmetic sign
theorem.
