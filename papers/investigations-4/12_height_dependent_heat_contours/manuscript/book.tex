\begin{abstract}
A holomorphic backward-heat field admits a smooth complex amplitude
whose squared modulus is a regularized zero-counting density. We derive
its phase transport, a nonlinear Schr\"odinger--Poisson evolution, and
its exact identification with a focusing Davey--Stewartson II system.
The emphasis is analytic: every field identity and conservation law used
here is calculated in the stated normalization.

For the potential selected by a translation-invariant Fourier multiplier,
we prove conservation of mass, two momenta and an indefinite Hamiltonian,
together with first-moment and mixed-virial identities under the required
integrability assumptions. A separate pullback calculation gives a family
of local conservation laws for the holomorphic lift. The area formula
classifies the amplitude's total mass: it equals the degree for a
polynomial and is infinite for a transcendental entire function. Thus
the theta heat field requires localization or a change of weighting.
Polynomial heat fields have zero momenta and zero Hamiltonian, including
at multiple zeros, and provide explicit controls on the meaning of these
invariants.

A self-subtracted square of a spatially smoothed density converges to
the multiplicity excess $\sum m(m-1)$. We calculate its scale dependence,
transport law and a quantitative measure-comparison bound. This
observable is not conserved. The resulting formulation supplies an
evolution and multiplicity-sensitive measurements; a useful
theta-specific upper bound remains open.
\end{abstract}

\section{The field and the question}
\label{sec:setup}

Let $t$ be real time and $z=x+iy$ the complex spatial variable. Suppose
$H(t,z)$ is holomorphic in $z$, smooth in $t$, has no constant spatial
slice, and satisfies
\begin{equation}
 H_t=-H_{zz}. \label{eq:heat}
\end{equation}
All local calculations are on regions where these hypotheses hold.
Global results state their extra hypotheses separately. Fix a constant
$\eps>0$ and put
\begin{equation}
 D=|H|^2+\eps^2,\qquad
 A=\frac{\eps}{\sqrt\pi}\frac{H_z}{D},\qquad
 \rho=|A|^2,\qquad V=-2\partial_{xy}\log D.
 \label{eq:definition}
\end{equation}
Dependence on $\eps$ is suppressed in $A,\rho,V$ when no confusion is
possible. The positive denominator makes all three fields smooth,
including at multiple zeros of $H$.

The amplitude $A$ is the primary field in the evolution below. A
normalized observation state will be denoted $\psi$, and a real spatial
cutoff will be denoted $h$. These objects have different roles. A
probability density is $|\psi|^2$, while its complex phase supplies
transport information. A moving spatial wall is not required to define
either field.

The canonical application is
\begin{align}
 H(t,z)&=\int_0^\infty e^{tu^2}\Phi(u)\cos(zu)\dd u,
 \label{eq:theta}\\
 \Phi(u)&=\sum_{n\ge1}
 (2\pi^2n^4e^{9u}-3\pi n^2e^{5u})e^{-\pi n^2e^{4u}},
 &H(0,z)&=\tfrac18\xi(\tfrac12+iz/2).
 \label{eq:source}
\end{align}
Here $\xi(s)=\tfrac12s(s-1)\pi^{-s/2}\Gamma(s/2)\zeta(s)$;
the convention agrees with \cite{polymath}. In this coordinate, real
$z$ corresponds to the critical line. The source is positive and
superexponentially decaying. Differentiation under its integral proves
\eqref{eq:heat} and joint analyticity. No Riemann hypothesis is needed
for the field identities in this paper.

The construction is a reformulation of holomorphic heat evolution and
its zero density, with a connection to established DS-II theory
\cite{KS,Taimanov}. It is not a claim of a new integrable system or a
proof of simplicity. Our question is which exact equations,
conserved quantities and observables remain available when
multiplicities are allowed.

\section{Zero density and the global mass classification}
\label{sec:mass}

\subsection{Local counting with a smooth amplitude}

\begin{proposition}[Density identity]
The amplitude \eqref{eq:definition} satisfies
\begin{equation}
 \rho=\frac{\eps^2|H_z|^2}{\pi D^2}
      =\frac1{4\pi}\Delta\log D. \label{eq:lapdensity}
\end{equation}
For fixed $t$, its measures converge on compact sets as
\begin{equation}
 \rho\dd x\dd y\ \rightharpoonup\
 \sum_{H(t,a)=0}m_a\delta_a\qquad(\eps\downarrow0).
 \label{eq:divisor}
\end{equation}
Here $m_a$ is the multiplicity (order) of the zero of $H(t,\cdot)$
at $a$, and $\delta_a$ is the unit point mass at $a$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Write $f=H$ and $p=H_z$. The Cauchy--Riemann equations give
\[
 \Delta D=4|p|^2,\qquad |\nabla D|^2=4|f|^2|p|^2.
\]
The quotient rule for $\Delta\log D$ proves
\eqref{eq:lapdensity}. For the measure limit, set
\begin{equation}
 k_\eps(w)=\frac{\eps^2}{\pi(|w|^2+\eps^2)^2},
 \qquad \int_\C k_\eps(w)\dd A_w=1.
 \label{eq:targetkernel}
\end{equation}
The holomorphic area formula uses the Jacobian $|H_z|^2$.
On a small disk containing just an order-$m$ zero, every sufficiently
small regular target value has $m$ preimages, counted with multiplicity;
this follows from Rouch\'e's theorem. All those preimages approach the
zero as the target approaches zero. Integration against the approximate
identity $k_\eps$ gives mass $m$ and the appropriate value of each
continuous test function. On the remaining compact set, $|H|$ has a
positive lower bound and the density tends uniformly to zero. A finite
disk cover proves \eqref{eq:divisor}.
\end{proof}

For the exact local model $H=c(z-a)^m$, radial integration gives
\begin{equation}
 \int_{|z-a|<R}\rho\dd A
 =\frac{m|c|^2R^{2m}}{|c|^2R^{2m}+\eps^2}. \label{eq:monomial}
\end{equation}
At a simple zero, $A(a)=H_z(a)/(\sqrt\pi\eps)$ is finite and
nonzero for fixed $\eps$. At a multiple zero, $A(a)=0$. For $m>1$,
the model density peaks on the circle
\[
 |z-a|^{2m}=\frac{m-1}{m+1}\frac{\eps^2}{|c|^2}.
\]
Thus a multiple zero can be represented by a ring of density around a
zero of the amplitude. Multiplicity is the limiting integrated mass;
it need not be the height of a pointwise density maximum.

The regulator measures function values. The spatial scale near a simple
zero is approximately $\eps/|H_z(a)|$, and near the displayed
order-$m$ model it is $(\eps/|c|)^{1/m}$. Fixed $\eps$ does not give
fixed spatial resolution at different heights. A space- or
time-dependent regulator would add terms to the subsequent equations.

\subsection{Finite mass is exactly the polynomial case}

\begin{theorem}[Total mass of an entire slice]
\label{thm:massclassification}
Let $f$ be a nonconstant entire function and let $A$ be
\eqref{eq:definition} with $H=f$. For every $\eps>0$,
\begin{equation}
 \int_\C |A|^2\dd A_z=
 \begin{cases}
 n,&f\text{ is a polynomial of degree }n,\\
 +\infty,&f\text{ is transcendental entire}.
 \end{cases} \label{eq:massclassification}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Apply the area formula first on increasing disks and then use monotone
convergence. If $n_f(w)$ is the number of preimages of $w$, it gives
\begin{equation}
 \int_\C |A|^2\dd A_z
 =\int_\C k_\eps(w)n_f(w)\dd A_w. \label{eq:globalarea}
\end{equation}
A degree-$n$ polynomial has $n_f(w)=n$ at every regular value, giving
the first case.

A transcendental entire function has an essential singularity at
infinity. Great Picard's theorem, in the form stated in
\cite[\S1.10(iii)]{DLMFPicard}, implies that at most one complex value
can have only finitely many preimages: if two did, a sufficiently large
exterior disk would omit both. Hence $n_f(w)=\infty$ for almost every
$w$. Since $k_\eps$ is strictly positive, \eqref{eq:globalarea} is
infinite. Critical values form a null set and do not affect the area
formula or either conclusion.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[The canonical field has infinite mass]
For every real $t$ and every constant $\eps>0$, the amplitude constructed
from \eqref{eq:theta} satisfies $\int_\C|A|^2=\infty$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The Riemann--Lebesgue lemma applies to the integrable source
$e^{tu^2}\Phi(u)$, so $H(t,x)\to0$ as real $|x|\to\infty$.
Positivity gives $H(t,0)>0$. Thus the entire slice cannot be a
polynomial, and Theorem~\ref{thm:massclassification} applies.
\end{proof}

This is a statement about the specific amplitude \eqref{eq:definition},
not about all possible weighted zero fields. It proves that a global
constant normalization of this amplitude is impossible. It does not
preclude a finite-window state, treated in Section~\ref{sec:window}.
Unfolding height to constant mean zero spacing cannot turn an infinite
count into finite total mass.

\section{Phase, zero velocity and local conservation}
\label{sec:transport}

We write planar velocities and currents as complex-valued fields.
For any smooth field, use the Wirtinger derivative
$\partial_z=\tfrac12(\partial_x-i\partial_y)$; on holomorphic $H$
this agrees with its holomorphic derivative. For a complex current
$j=u+iv$, its divergence is $2\Re(\partial_z j)=u_x+v_y$.
For a real function $f$, the directional pairing is
$2\Re((\partial_z f)j)=f_xu+f_yv$.

At a simple zero $a(t)$, the implicit-function theorem and
\eqref{eq:heat} give
\begin{equation}
 \dot a=\frac{H_{zz}}{H_z}(t,a). \label{eq:zerovelocity}
\end{equation}
Where $H_z\ne0$, choose a local phase $\theta=\arg A=\arg H_z$.
Holomorphicity yields
\begin{equation}
 \theta_x=\Im\frac{H_{zz}}{H_z},\qquad
 \theta_y=\Re\frac{H_{zz}}{H_z},\qquad
 v=\theta_y+i\theta_x=\frac{H_{zz}}{H_z}.
 \label{eq:phasevelocity}
\end{equation}
Thus the phase gradient supplies the zero velocity with its coordinate
derivatives exchanged. The density gradient alone does not determine
transport.

\begin{proposition}[Smooth conserved current]
Define
\begin{align}
 j&:=\Im(\bar A A_y)+i\Im(\bar A A_x),
 \label{eq:current}\\
 j&=\frac{\eps^2H_{zz}\overline{H_z}}{\pi D^2}.
 \label{eq:currentheat}
\end{align}
The real part of $j$ is the $x$-current and its imaginary part is
the $y$-current; $j$ is generally not holomorphic.
Then everywhere, including at critical and multiple points,
\begin{equation}
 \rho_t+2\Re(\partial_z j)=0.
 \label{eq:continuity}
\end{equation}
On the regular set, $j=\rho v$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The derivatives of the positive denominator in \eqref{eq:definition}
contribute only real terms to $\bar A A_x$ and $\bar A A_y$.
Their imaginary parts therefore give \eqref{eq:currentheat} and
\eqref{eq:phasevelocity}. For an independent geometric proof of the
continuity equation, pull back the closed target-plane two-form
$k_\eps(w)\dd\Re w\wedge\dd\Im w$ by $H(t,z)$. Its pullback is
\[
 \rho\dd x\wedge\dd y+\Im(j)\dd t\wedge\dd x
 -\Re(j)\dd t\wedge\dd y.
\]
Its exterior derivative is
$(\rho_t+2\Re(\partial_z j))\dd t\wedge\dd x\wedge\dd y$,
which must vanish. No quotient by $H_z$ is used at critical points.
\end{proof}

The field and current remain smooth at fixed $\eps$ even when
individual zero velocities diverge. An order-$m$ zero of $H$ produces
local phase winding $m-1$ in $A$. However, $A$ also vanishes at critical
points with $H\ne0$. Winding alone does not identify a zero of $H$;
it must be combined with the density or the holomorphic lift.

\begin{proposition}[Weighted target laws]
For every fixed smooth real function $g$ on the target plane,
\begin{equation}
 \partial_t\{g(H)\rho\}
 +2\Re\bigl(\partial_z\{g(H)j\}\bigr)=0.
 \label{eq:targetlaws}
\end{equation}
Consequently $\int g(H)\rho$ is conserved whenever it is integrable
and the current has no net boundary contribution. For a degree-$n$
polynomial and bounded $g$ it equals
\begin{equation}
 n\int_\C g(w)k_\eps(w)\dd A_w. \label{eq:targetcharges}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Replace the target area form above by
$g(w)k_\eps(w)\dd\Re w\wedge\dd\Im w$, which is still closed.
The same pullback proves the local identity. Integrate it with its
boundary flux retained. Formula \eqref{eq:targetcharges} follows from
the area formula.
\end{proof}

These are conservation laws of the holomorphic lift $(H,A)$; they use
$H$ as well as $A$. They are not asserted for every solution of the
larger field equation below. In the polynomial case the charges in
\eqref{eq:targetcharges} depend only on degree and the target weight,
and therefore do not distinguish a simple configuration from a
multiple one of the same degree.

\section{The exact nonlinear field equation}
\label{sec:evolution}

\begin{theorem}[Schr\"odinger--Poisson evolution]
\label{thm:evolution}
The fields \eqref{eq:definition} satisfy
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\quad iA_t=-A_{xy}+VA,\qquad
 \Delta V=-8\pi\partial_{xy}|A|^2.\quad}
 \label{eq:SP}
\end{equation}
Both identities hold smoothly through zeros of $H$ and $H_z$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Write $f=H$, $p=H_z$, $q=H_{zz}$, $r=H_{zzz}$ and
$c=\eps/\sqrt\pi$. Then
\begin{align*}
 D_x&=p\bar f+f\bar p,&D_y&=i(p\bar f-f\bar p),\\
 D_t&=-q\bar f-f\bar q,&D_{xy}&=i(q\bar f-f\bar q),\\
 p_t&=-r,&p_x&=q,\quad p_y=iq,\quad p_{xy}=ir.
\end{align*}
Differentiating $A=cp/D$ gives
\begin{align*}
 A_t&=c\left(-\frac rD-\frac{pD_t}{D^2}\right),\\
 A_{xy}&=c\left(\frac{ir}D
 -\frac{q(D_y+iD_x)+pD_{xy}}{D^2}
 +\frac{2pD_xD_y}{D^3}\right).
\end{align*}
Since $D_y+iD_x=2ip\bar f$ and
$-iD_t-2iq\bar f=-D_{xy}$, cancellation gives
\[
 iA_t+A_{xy}
 =-2\left(\frac{D_{xy}}D-\frac{D_xD_y}{D^2}\right)A=VA.
\]
Applying $-2\partial_{xy}$ to
$\Delta\log D=4\pi\rho$ proves the second equation.
\end{proof}

An equivalent useful formula is
\begin{equation}
 V=4\Im\left(\frac{\bar H H_{zz}}D
             -\frac{\bar H^2 H_z^2}{D^2}\right).
 \label{eq:explicitV}
\end{equation}
No derivative quotient occurs in this expression. For any smooth
solution of the first equation in \eqref{eq:SP} with real $V$, taking
the real part of $2\bar A A_t$ proves
\eqref{eq:continuity} with the current \eqref{eq:current}.

\subsection{Davey--Stewartson II coordinates}

Set
\[
 X=\frac{x+y}{\sqrt2},\quad Y=\frac{y-x}{\sqrt2},\quad t=2\tau,
 \quad q(\tau,X,Y)=2\sqrt\pi A(2\tau,x,y).
\]
Writing $V^\#(\tau,X,Y)=V(2\tau,x,y)$ and
$\Phi=V^\#-|q|^2$, the identities
$\partial_{xy}=\tfrac12(\partial_{XX}-\partial_{YY})$ and
$\Delta=\partial_{XX}+\partial_{YY}$ give
\begin{equation}
 \begin{split}
 iq_\tau+q_{XX}-q_{YY}-2(\Phi+|q|^2)q&=0,\\
 \Delta\Phi+2\partial_{XX}|q|^2&=0.
 \end{split} \label{eq:DSII}
\end{equation}
This is the focusing DS-II convention in \cite[equation (1)]{KS}.
Related constructions from holomorphic Schr\"odinger seeds and
two-dimensional Dirac operators occur in \cite{Taimanov}; its displayed
Moutard formula is not identified with \eqref{eq:definition}.
Only the differential-system comparison is imported here. We derive
the conservation statements used below directly, without importing a
global existence or finite-mass theorem for theta.

\subsection{Selecting the potential}

The Poisson equation determines $V$ only up to a harmonic function.
For the finite-mass theory we specify a real, translation-invariant
operator by
\begin{equation}
 \widehat{\cT f}(k_x,k_y)
 =-8\pi\frac{k_xk_y}{k_x^2+k_y^2}\widehat f(k_x,k_y),
 \qquad k\ne0. \label{eq:multiplier}
\end{equation}
Its value at $k=0$ is irrelevant in $L^2$. This bounded, selfadjoint
operator has norm at most $4\pi$, commutes with derivatives and
translations, and is homogeneous of degree zero. The closed equation
in the next section is
\begin{equation}
 iA_t=-A_{xy}+(\cT\rho)A,\qquad \rho=|A|^2.
 \label{eq:closed}
\end{equation}
The holomorphic formula supplies its own potential; identifying that
potential with $\cT\rho$ requires global hypotheses. These are proved
for polynomials below and are not assumed for theta. Moreover, an
arbitrary solution of \eqref{eq:closed} need not admit the lift
\eqref{eq:definition}, much less the unchanged theta source.

\section{Conserved quantities and their hypotheses}
\label{sec:conservation}

In this section suppose $A$ solves \eqref{eq:closed} smoothly on a
time interval and has enough decay to justify the indicated integrals,
differentiation and integrations by parts. A smooth rapidly decreasing
$A$ suffices. The polynomial fields of Section~\ref{sec:polynomial}
have slower explicit decay, for which the same steps are verified.
These are conditional conservation theorems for solutions, not
existence theorems or assertions of global finiteness for theta.

Define momentum densities, total mass and momenta by
\begin{equation}
 p_x=\Im(\bar A A_x),\quad p_y=\Im(\bar A A_y),\qquad
 M=\int\rho,\quad P_x=\int p_x,\quad P_y=\int p_y.
 \label{eq:charges}
\end{equation}
All unmarked integrals in this section are over $\R^2$ with measure
$\dd x\dd y$. The complex current is $j=p_y+ip_x$.
Set
\begin{equation}
 K=\int\Re(\overline{A_x}A_y),\qquad
 U=\int\rho\cT\rho,\qquad
 \boxed{\cE=K+\tfrac12U.} \label{eq:energy}
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Mass, momentum and Hamiltonian]
\label{thm:conservation}
Under these hypotheses, $M$, $P_x$, $P_y$ and $\cE$ are independent
of time.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Mass conservation is the integral of \eqref{eq:continuity}.
For $i=x,y$, differentiating $p_i$ using a general real potential gives
\begin{align}
 (p_i)_t
 &=\Re(\bar A A_{ixy}-\overline{A_{xy}}A_i)-\rho V_i\notag\\
 &=\partial_x\Re(\bar A A_{iy})
   -\partial_y\Re(\overline{A_x}A_i)-\rho V_i.
 \label{eq:momentumlocal}
\end{align}
The divergence terms have zero integral. For $V=\cT\rho$,
selfadjointness and commutation with derivatives imply
\[
 \int\rho\partial_i\cT\rho
 =\int(\cT\rho)\partial_i\rho
 =-\int\rho\partial_i\cT\rho=0.
\]
This proves conservation of both momenta.

For the energy, integrations by parts and selfadjointness give
\begin{align*}
 \frac{dK}{dt}&=2\Re\int\overline{-A_{xy}}A_t,\\
 \frac12\frac{dU}{dt}
 &=\int V\rho_t=2\Re\int\overline{VA}A_t.
\end{align*}
Their sum is $2\Re\int\overline{iA_t}A_t=0$ by
\eqref{eq:closed}. The factor $1/2$ in the potential energy is
necessary: the potential depends on the density and is differentiated
along with it.
\end{proof}

The variational derivative is
\[
 \frac{\delta\cE}{\delta\bar A}=-A_{xy}+(\cT\rho)A.
\]
Accordingly the action
\begin{equation}
 \int\left\{\int\frac i2(\bar A A_t-A\bar A_t)-\cE[A]\right\}\dd t
 \label{eq:action}
\end{equation}
has \eqref{eq:closed} as its Euler--Lagrange equation. Its phase,
translation and time symmetries agree with the three preceding
calculations. This action is nonlocal in space through $\cT$.

\begin{remark}[The Hamiltonian is indefinite]
The multiplier of $-\partial_{xy}$ is $k_xk_y$, which takes both
signs, and $|U|\le4\pi\|A\|_4^4$. For a small amplitude modulated
real radial test function $A=\delta g(x,y)e^{i(k_xx+k_yy)}$,
$K=\delta^2 k_xk_y\|g\|_2^2$, while $U=O(\delta^4)$.
Choosing either sign of $k_xk_y$ gives either sign of $\cE$.
Conservation therefore does not provide a positive gradient bound.
\end{remark}

\subsection{First moments and mixed virial identities}

\begin{theorem}[Explicit time-dependent charges]
\label{thm:virial}
Assume additionally the moments and boundary terms in the following
calculation are finite. Define
\[
 C_x=\int x\rho,\quad C_y=\int y\rho,\quad
 \cD=\int(xp_x+yp_y),\quad \cB=\int xy\rho.
\]
Then
\begin{equation}
 \dot C_x=P_y,\qquad\dot C_y=P_x,\qquad
 \dot\cB=\cD,\qquad\dot\cD=2\cE.
 \label{eq:virial}
\end{equation}
In particular the quantities
\begin{equation}
 C_x-tP_y,\quad C_y-tP_x,\quad
 \cD-2t\cE,\quad \cB-t\cD+t^2\cE
 \label{eq:timecharges}
\end{equation}
are conserved.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Integrating the continuity equation against $x$, $y$ and $xy$ gives
the first three identities. Multiply \eqref{eq:momentumlocal} by
$x$ for $i=x$ and by $y$ for $i=y$, then integrate and add:
\begin{equation}
 \dot\cD=2K-\int\rho(xV_x+yV_y). \label{eq:dilationstep}
\end{equation}
Write $R=x\partial_x+y\partial_y$. Degree-zero homogeneity of
\eqref{eq:multiplier} gives $R\cT=\cT R$. Thus, with
$I=\int\rho RV$,
\[
 I=\int\rho\cT(R\rho)=\int V R\rho=-2\int V\rho-I.
\]
Hence $I=-U$, and \eqref{eq:dilationstep} becomes
$\dot\cD=2K+U=2\cE$. Differentiate the expressions in
\eqref{eq:timecharges} to finish the proof.
\end{proof}

The virial here is the signed mixed moment $xy$, not the positive
variance $x^2+y^2$. Thus $\ddot\cB=2\cE$ has no automatic
positive-variance consequence. The time-dependent charges are not
independent claims that $C_x$, $C_y$, $\cD$ or $\cB$ themselves
are stationary.

\subsection{Boundary forcing and the potential ambiguity}

For a fixed bounded region $\Omega$, write its outward unit normal
as the complex number $n=n_x+in_y$. The universally valid mass law is
\begin{equation}
 \frac d{dt}\int_\Omega\rho
 =-\int_{\partial\Omega}\Re(j\bar n)\dd s.
 \label{eq:boundarymass}
\end{equation}
The local momentum balance \eqref{eq:momentumlocal} similarly retains
its two boundary stresses and the force $-\rho\nabla V$.

Even on the whole plane, adding harmonic data changes the available
invariants. If $V=\cT\rho+W(t,x,y)$, with real prescribed $W$ and
integrable terms, the same calculation gives
\begin{equation}
 \dot P_i=-\int\rho W_i,\qquad
 \frac d{dt}\left(\cE+\int W\rho\right)=\int W_t\rho.
 \label{eq:forcing}
\end{equation}
For the original Poisson equation $W$ must be harmonic, but these
identities hold for any real external potential. It is therefore
essential to specify the potential and the boundary conditions before
claiming conserved global energy or momentum.

\section{Exact polynomial calculations and collision controls}
\label{sec:polynomial}

Let
\begin{equation}
 H(t,z)=\sum_{k=0}^n a_k(t)z^k,\qquad n\ge1,\quad a_n\ne0,
 \label{eq:polynomial}
\end{equation}
solve \eqref{eq:heat}. Its degree and leading two coefficients are
constant, and for $n\ge2$,
\begin{equation}
 \dot a_{n-2}=-n(n-1)a_n. \label{eq:coefficient}
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Charges on the polynomial sector]
\label{thm:polycharges}
For every such polynomial heat field and every $\eps>0$, the
amplitude in \eqref{eq:definition} has
\begin{equation}
 M=n,\qquad P_x=P_y=0,\qquad\cE=0.
 \label{eq:polycharges}
\end{equation}
The angular moment $L=\int(xp_y-yp_x)$, not asserted conserved for
general solutions of \eqref{eq:closed}, has the fixed value
\begin{equation}
 L=n(n-1). \label{eq:polyangular}
\end{equation}
For $n\ge2$, the complex moments are
\begin{align}
 \int z\rho\dd A&=-\frac{a_{n-1}}{a_n},\label{eq:firstmoment}\\
 \int z^2\rho\dd A
 &=\left(\frac{a_{n-1}}{a_n}\right)^2
    -\frac{2a_{n-2}(t)}{a_n}.
 \label{eq:secondmoment}
\end{align}
The first-moment formula also holds for $n=1$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Uniformly on compact time intervals, $A=O(|z|^{-n-1})$,
$\nabla A=O(|z|^{-n-2})$, $A_t=O(|z|^{-n-3})$ and
$V=O(|z|^{-2})$ at infinity. Their further derivatives have the
corresponding bounds. These justify mass, momentum and energy
calculations. The second spatial moment is absolutely integrable for
$n\ge2$. Also $V,\rho\in L^2$ and $\Delta(V-\cT\rho)=0$.
An entire harmonic $L^2$ function is zero, so the potential agrees
with \eqref{eq:multiplier}.

Theorem~\ref{thm:massclassification} gives $M=n$. For the other
moments, use the area formula with the spatial weights $z,z^2$.
For $n\ge2$, the sum of roots of $H(z)=w$ is
$-a_{n-1}/a_n$. For $n\ge3$, their squared sum is the right-hand side
of \eqref{eq:secondmoment}. For $n=2$, an additional term $2w/a_n$
occurs. Since
\[
 \int_\C w k_\eps(w)\dd A_w=0,
 \qquad \int_\C |w|k_\eps(w)\dd A_w<\infty,
\]
the additional term integrates to zero. The same first target moment
proves \eqref{eq:firstmoment} for the linear polynomial. Absolute
integrability justifies these applications of Fubini.

The first complex moment is stationary, so
\eqref{eq:virial} implies $P_x=P_y=0$. For $n\ge2$,
\eqref{eq:coefficient}--\eqref{eq:secondmoment} give
\begin{equation}
 \frac d{dt}\int z^2\rho\dd A=2n(n-1)\in\R.
 \label{eq:secondderivative}
\end{equation}
Its imaginary part says that $\cB=\tfrac12\Im\int z^2\rho$
is constant. Then $\cD=0$ and $\cE=0$ follow from
Theorem~\ref{thm:virial}. Its real part, together with continuity,
is
\[
 \frac d{dt}\int(x^2-y^2)\rho
 =2\int(xp_y-yp_x)=2L,
\]
which proves \eqref{eq:polyangular}.

For $n=1$ the field is stationary and has constant phase. Both momenta
and $L$ are zero. Its density is radial about the single zero;
the kinetic and potential energy integrals each vanish by oddness in
the centered coordinates. This gives $\cE=0$ without using a
divergent second spatial moment.
\end{proof}

These values are independent of the number of distinct zeros. In
particular, take
\begin{equation}
 H(t,z)=z^2-2t,\qquad
 A=\frac{2\eps z}{\sqrt\pi(|z^2-2t|^2+\eps^2)}.
 \label{eq:doublecontrol}
\end{equation}
For every real $t$, $M=2$, $P_x=P_y=\cE=0$ and $L=2$.
The globally normalized state $A/\sqrt2$ is smooth at the double
zero at $t=0$. The two zeros are nonreal for $t<0$ and real simple
for $t>0$. Therefore these conserved quantities and smooth normalized
evolution admit a change in multiplicity and in real-zero population.

The same local field identities hold for the repository's positive-source
collision controls because they solve holomorphic heat evolution.
Finite localization is available for each one. They fail no hypothesis
of the generic field construction. Transcendental controls fail the
finite-global-mass hypothesis, just as theta does; the polynomial
control satisfies that additional hypothesis. What distinguishes theta is its
unchanged complete arithmetic source, which has not yet supplied a
quantitative bound on the observable developed below.

\section{Normalized local states and their exact corrections}
\label{sec:window}

Choose a fixed real $h\in C_c^\infty(\C)$, nonnegative and equal
to one on the finite region of interest. For a nonconstant entire
slice and nonzero $h$, define
\begin{equation}
 Z(t)=\int h^2\rho\dd A>0,\qquad
 \psi=\frac{hA}{\sqrt Z},\qquad \int|\psi|^2\dd A=1.
 \label{eq:localstate}
\end{equation}
The phase agrees with that of $A$ wherever $h>0$. Retain $Z$
alongside the state, since norm one alone does not record absolute
counting mass.

\begin{proposition}[Localization and normalization]
Put $J=h^2j/Z$. Then
\begin{align}
 \dot Z&=\int 4h\Re(h_zj)\dd A,
 \label{eq:Zdot}\\
 \partial_t|\psi|^2+2\Re(\partial_z J)
 &=\frac{4h\Re(h_zj)}{Z}
   -\frac{\dot Z}{Z}|\psi|^2,\label{eq:probability}\\
 i\psi_t&=-\psi_{xy}+V\psi
 +\frac{h_{xy}A+h_xA_y+h_yA_x}{\sqrt Z}
 -i\frac{\dot Z}{2Z}\psi.\label{eq:psievolution}
\end{align}
Here $h_z=\partial_z h$. The complex current $J$ is also obtained
from $\psi$ using
\eqref{eq:current}.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Multiply continuity by $h^2$ and integrate by parts to obtain
\eqref{eq:Zdot}. The product rule applied before integration gives
\eqref{eq:probability}. Expand $(hA)_{xy}$ and differentiate
$Z^{-1/2}$ to obtain \eqref{eq:psievolution}. The terms containing
$h_x$ or $h_y$ in $\bar\psi\psi_x$ or $\bar\psi\psi_y$ are real
and do not change the current. None of these formulas divides by $h$.
\end{proof}

The state remains regular at the edge of its support. It satisfies an
equation with explicit localization and normalization terms, rather than
the unmodified whole-plane equation. Its potential is still supplied
by the complete field; replacing it by $\cT|\psi|^2$ would define a
different evolution. This is particularly relevant for theta, where
the unlocalized mass is infinite.

For an approximate amplitude with real potential, define
\begin{equation}
 r=iA_t+A_{xy}-VA.
\end{equation}
The exact continuity defect is
\begin{equation}
 \rho_t+2\Re(\partial_z j)=2\Im(\bar A r),\qquad
 \left|\dot Z-\int4h\Re(h_zj)\dd A\right|
 \le2\|hA\|_2\|hr\|_2.
 \label{eq:residual}
\end{equation}
This follows by substituting the residual in the calculation of
continuity and using Cauchy--Schwarz. A residual in the potential
equation must be tracked separately. Normalization cannot remove
either evolution error.

\section{A multiplicity observable from smoothed density squares}
\label{sec:observable}

The mass counts zeros with multiplicity but is conserved through
collisions. To distinguish coincident zeros from separated zeros,
consider a square of a spatially smoothed density. The semigroup
factorization behind this calculation is the useful feature of the
earlier continuum-sigma notes \cite{BakerSigma}; its self-contribution
must be subtracted.

\subsection{The finite-divisor identity}

Fix a nonempty finite divisor $\nu=\sum_a m_a\delta_a$, with distinct support
points $a$, and let $N=\nu(\C)$. Define
\begin{equation}
 K_s(z)=\frac1{4\pi s}e^{-|z|^2/(4s)},\qquad
 \rho_s=K_s*\nu,\qquad s>0.
 \label{eq:spatialkernel}
\end{equation}
This spatial smoothing scale $s$ is distinct from the value-plane
regulator $\eps$.

\begin{theorem}[Self-subtracted pair observable]
\label{thm:pairs}
One has
\begin{align}
 Q_s(\nu)&:=8\pi s\int_\C\rho_s^2\dd A-N\notag\\
 &=\sum_a m_a(m_a-1)
   +\sum_{a\ne b}m_am_b e^{-|a-b|^2/(8s)}.
 \label{eq:pairidentity}
\end{align}
The second sum is over ordered pairs. In particular
\begin{equation}
 \lim_{s\downarrow0}Q_s(\nu)=\sum_a m_a(m_a-1).
 \label{eq:excess}
\end{equation}
If distinct support points are separated by at least $d>0$, then
\begin{equation}
 0\le Q_s(\nu)-\sum_a m_a(m_a-1)
 \le\left(N^2-\sum_a m_a^2\right)e^{-d^2/(8s)}.
 \label{eq:separation}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The Gaussian semigroup law gives
\[
 \int K_s(z-a)K_s(z-b)\dd A_z
 =K_{2s}(a-b)=\frac1{8\pi s}e^{-|a-b|^2/(8s)}.
\]
Expand the square of the finite sum defining $\rho_s$. The diagonal
terms give $\sum m_a^2$; subtracting $N=\sum m_a$ leaves the first
sum in \eqref{eq:pairidentity}. The finite off-diagonal sum tends to
zero and obeys \eqref{eq:separation}.
\end{proof}

For $p_s=\rho_s/N$, the same expression is
$8\pi sN^2\int p_s^2-N$. If $p_s=|\psi_s|^2$, it is a quartic
observable of a normalized amplitude with its mass factor retained.
The smoothed amplitude $\psi_s$ need not have the phase or evolution
of \eqref{eq:definition}; the observable can instead be applied directly
to that field's density.

\subsection{Recovering the observable from the smooth field}

At a fixed time choose $h$ to equal one near every selected zero, with
no zeros in its transition region and no unselected zero in its support.
Put $\mu_\eps=h^2\rho\dd A$ and $Z_\eps=\mu_\eps(\C)$.
Then the directly measurable expression is
\begin{equation}
 Q_{s,\eps}=8\pi s\int
 \left[K_s*(Z_\eps|\psi|^2)\right]^2\dd A-Z_\eps.
 \label{eq:fieldobservable}
\end{equation}
For every fixed $s>0$, \eqref{eq:divisor} gives
$\mu_\eps\rightharpoonup\nu$ and $Z_\eps\to N$ on a common compact
support. The continuous bounded pair kernel therefore gives
\begin{equation}
 \lim_{s\downarrow0}\lim_{\eps\downarrow0}Q_{s,\eps}
 =\sum_a m_a(m_a-1). \label{eq:orderedlimit}
\end{equation}
This can also be proved by approximating the pair kernel uniformly by
finite sums of products of continuous functions on the compact support.

The order matters. At fixed $\eps>0$ the localized density is smooth
and square integrable, so $K_s*(h^2\rho)\to h^2\rho$ in $L^2$ as
$s\downarrow0$. Consequently $Q_{s,\eps}\to-Z_\eps$. Thus
\eqref{eq:fieldobservable} is not automatically nonnegative at finite
regulator. Fractional cutoff values at zeros would also replace the
integer multiplicities by weighted masses.

\begin{proposition}[A quantitative comparison at fixed scale]
\label{prop:W1}
Use the same definition of $Q_s$ for positive finite measures.
If $\mu,\nu$ have equal finite mass $N>0$ and finite
first moments, then
\begin{equation}
 |Q_s(\mu)-Q_s(\nu)|
 \le\frac{N e^{-1/2}}{\sqrt s}W_1(\mu,\nu),
 \label{eq:W1}
\end{equation}
where $W_1$ is the transport cost for measures of mass $N$, without
normalizing them to probability measures. If $\mu(\C)=Z>0$ and
$\widehat\mu=(N/Z)\mu$, then
\begin{equation}
 |Q_s(\mu)-Q_s(\nu)|
 \le(Z+N+1)|Z-N|
   +\frac{N e^{-1/2}}{\sqrt s}W_1(\widehat\mu,\nu).
 \label{eq:W1mass}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Write $G_s(z)=e^{-|z|^2/(8s)}$. Its maximum gradient is
$e^{-1/2}/(2\sqrt s)$, attained at $|z|=2\sqrt s$.
For any coupling of $\mu$ and $\nu$, take its product with itself.
The difference of the two pair kernels is at most this Lipschitz
constant times the sum of the two displacement lengths. Integration
gives $2N$ times the single transport cost, proving \eqref{eq:W1}.
For unequal masses, $0\le\iint G_s\dd\widehat\mu\dd\widehat\mu\le N^2$.
Rescaling the quadratic term changes it by at most
$(Z+N)|Z-N|$, and rescaling the subtracted mass changes it by
$|Z-N|$. Apply \eqref{eq:W1} to $\widehat\mu,\nu$.
\end{proof}

This bound states the resolution cost explicitly. It does not supply a
theta-specific estimate for $W_1$ or the mass error as height grows.

\subsection{The observable evolves; it is not a conserved charge}

For differentiable distinct tracks $a(t)$ with fixed multiplicities,
differentiate \eqref{eq:pairidentity}:
\begin{equation}
 \frac d{dt}Q_s
 =-\frac1{4s}\sum_{a\ne b}m_am_b
 \Re\bigl(\overline{a-b}(\dot a-\dot b)\bigr)
 e^{-|a-b|^2/(8s)}.
 \label{eq:pairtime}
\end{equation}
The real part is the Euclidean pairing of the complex displacement
and relative velocity. There is no universal sign. For
\eqref{eq:doublecontrol}, including the coincident divisor
at $t=0$, the exact value is
\begin{equation}
 Q_s(t)=2e^{-|t|/s}. \label{eq:paircontrol}
\end{equation}
It increases toward the collision and decreases afterward, while all
charges in \eqref{eq:polycharges} remain fixed. The scale limit is not
uniform at the collision.

For the smooth field with a fixed cutoff, put
$m=h^2\rho$, $j_h=h^2j$ and $b_h=4h\Re(h_zj)$. Then
$m_t+2\Re(\partial_z j_h)=b_h$. Direct differentiation yields
\begin{align}
 \frac d{dt}Q_{s,\eps}
 &=32\pi s\Re\int
 \partial_z(K_s*m)(K_s*j_h)\dd A\notag\\
 &\quad+16\pi s\int(K_s*m)(K_s*b_h)\dd A-\int b_h\dd A.
 \label{eq:observabletransport}
\end{align}
The first term describes transport within the observed density, and
the last two retain the window's flux. The formula follows by moving
the divergence through convolution and integrating by parts against
the Gaussian-smoothed density. An approximate amplitude adds the
source $2h^2\Im(\bar A r)$ to $b_h$.

\section{What the formulation accomplishes}
\label{sec:scope}

The analytic conclusions separate into three levels. Locally, every
holomorphic heat field has the smooth amplitude, phase transport,
Schr\"odinger--Poisson equation and conserved current. With a selected
translation-invariant potential and sufficient global integrability,
mass, momenta, Hamiltonian and the stated moment combinations are
conserved. For a finite observed divisor, the self-subtracted density
square detects multiplicity in the ordered small-scale limit.

The canonical theta field belongs to the first level but has infinite
unlocalized mass at every positive regulator. Local normalization has
therefore been included as an exact part of the formulation. Infinite
mass alone does not decide whether some other signed global integral
converges; no finite global theta energy or momentum theorem is asserted
here. A fixed observation window suffices, with its flux retained.

The polynomial calculation shows why conservation alone is not the
desired simplicity mechanism. Degree, the two zero momenta, the zero
Hamiltonian, and even the restricted angular moment can coexist with
collisions. The observable $Q_s$ records the missing distinction, but
also responds to close distinct zeros. Its useful control would require
an estimate from the actual theta source, together with regulator,
localization and increasing-height costs. No such upper estimate or
new simplicity proportion is obtained here.
